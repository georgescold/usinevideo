When a dismissive avoidant and you both
stop contacting each other, this is what
happens next. It starts with silence.
The kind that isn't loud or dramatic,
just empty. Two people, both wounded in
their own way, both protecting
themselves in different styles of pain,
finally stop reaching out. The calls
end. The texts fade. The pulland push
cycle, the endless dance of connection
and retreat finally collapses into
nothingness. And at first you think this
silence is the end. But for both you and
the dismissive avoidant, it's not the
end. It's the beginning of something
deeper, something uncomfortable, and
something revealing. Because when two
people stop chasing, what's left isn't
peace. It's truth. When a dismissive
avoidance stops contacting you, it's
rarely about hatred or indifference.
It's usually about control. They believe
that by staying distant, they can avoid
vulnerability, avoid rejection, avoid
feeling the old wounds they've buried
for years. But when you stop contacting
them, too, something in their carefully
built emotional armor begins to crack.
At first, they feel relieved. Finally,
they think, "No more pressure." But as
the days pass, that relief transforms
into curiosity. The silence they once
used to protect themselves starts
echoing back at them. They wonder, "Why
isn't this person chasing me anymore?"
For someone with a dismissive, avoidant
attachment style, connection has always
been a battlefield. They crave intimacy
but fear it. They want love but
associate it with being controlled,
trapped, or hurt. So when you used to
reach out, when you texted, when you
checked in, when you tried to keep the
bond alive, it reinforced their sense of
security. They knew you'd always come
back. But when you stop contacting them
too, the power dynamic shifts. Suddenly,
they're not the one being chased.
They're the one wondering what happened.
And that confusion, it's the beginning
of their inner conflict. You, on the
other hand, go through your own wave of
emotions. At first, you feel the ache of
missing them. The habit of reaching out
is still strong. You catch yourself
checking your phone, wondering if
they'll finally message, wondering if
silence might pull them closer. But then
something changes after the initial pain
fades. A strange calm takes its place.
You start realizing how much emotional
energy you used to pour into trying to
maintain something that wasn't mutual.
And the silence that once felt like
punishment now starts feeling like
protection. You begin to reclaim
yourself piece by piece. But here's
where things get interesting. The
avoidant notices this energetic shift
even without contact. They may not
consciously admit it, but energetically
they sense that you're detaching. That
emotional cord they were so used to
tugging at for validation suddenly feels
weaker. And that triggers something deep
in them. Fear. The same fear they've
been running from their entire life. The
fear of being forgotten. Avoidance like
to believe they don't need anyone. But
when the person who once cared finally
goes quiet, they start feeling the very
abandonment they tried so hard to
prevent. At this stage, they might not
reach out directly. Instead, they start
testing the waters. They might view your
stories, like your old post, send a
random meme, or show up subtly in your
digital world, hoping to see if you'll
bite. Because while they don't want to
risk vulnerability, they also don't want
to feel replaced. It's their way of
saying, "Are you still there? Do you
still care? But here's the paradox. If
you respond too quickly, they retreat
again. If you stay detached, they start
spiraling in confusion. This is the
emotional tugof-war that begins once you
both stop contacting each other. Time,
however, becomes the great revealer.
After weeks or even months of no
contact, both of you begin to see
yourselves with more clarity. You begin
to see how much you were driven by
emotional need, the need to fix, to
prove your worth.
to earn love from someone who was never
fully open. The avoidant begins to feel
the weight of their own defenses. The
quiet forces them to sit with the
feelings they've long avoided.
Loneliness, regret, and the realization
that love requires vulnerability,
something they've been running from
their entire life. For some avoidance,
this silence becomes unbearable. They
may start reminiscing about the
connection, idealizing it, remembering
the warmth you gave them, the emotional
safety they didn't know they needed. But
pride keeps them frozen. They'll
convince themselves they're fine, that
they've moved on. Yet late at night,
when the world is still, their mind
drifts to you. They replay moments, your
laugh, your kindness, your patience, and
feel a strange ache they can't label.
That ache is recognition not just of you
but of what they lost through their
fear. Meanwhile, you start growing. You
start realizing that silence doesn't
have to mean loss. It can mean freedom.
You stop obsessing about their next move
and start focusing on your healing, your
boundaries, your emotional peace. You
realize you don't need closure from them
because silence is closure. It's their
way of showing you what they're capable
of giving and what they aren't. And in
that realization, something powerful
happens. You stop craving their
attention and you start valuing your own
energy. Months down the line, when both
of you have been silent for too long,
something shifts again. The avoidant may
reappear. Sometimes subtly, sometimes
dramatically. Maybe a, "Hey, how have
you been?" text. Maybe a random comment
on social media, or sometimes a
full-blown confession masked under
casual words. But it's never truly about
small talk. It's about testing if the
door is still open. Because deep down,
even avoidance crave emotional safety.
They just don't know how to create it.
When they sense you've moved on, it
stirs their deepest insecurity. That
they pushed away someone who actually
saw them. If you respond from your
healed space, you'll notice something
different. Their charm still exists, but
it doesn't hook you the same way. Their
distance no longer feels mysterious. It
feels predictable. You see their
patterns clearly now and you understand
that love cannot bloom where fear still
rules. And that's the moment you truly
gain power. Not by ignoring them, but by
understanding them without needing to
fix them. Because the truth is, healing
doesn't always mean reunion. Sometimes
it means recognition, seeing the pattern
for what it was, and choosing peace over
pain. And here's the twist most people

reaching out, not as a tactic, but as a
natural act of self-respect, that's when
the avoidant feels your absence most
deeply. Your silence becomes louder than
any message you could send. Because it's
no longer about punishing them. It's
about finally breaking free. And nothing

the person who once chased them is now

some cases, this silence becomes the

wounds. Questioning why intimacy feels
like suffocation and why love always
ends in withdrawal. It doesn't happen
overnight, but sometimes your absence

journey toward healing. For others,

But that emptiness always catches up

silence left by unresolved emotional
truth. So when you both stop contacting
each other, what really happens next
isn't just about who breaks the silence

For you, it's the awakening of your
self-worth. For them, it's the
confrontation of their fear. Sometimes
the silence brings reconciliation,
