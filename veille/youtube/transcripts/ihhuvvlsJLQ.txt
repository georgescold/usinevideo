# Are Dating Apps Designed to Keep You Single?
# Dr. Economist · 91 vues · 00:17:21
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ihhuvvlsJLQ

The economics behind dating apps is
interesting because the best possible
outcome for the user is also the worst
possible outcome for the app. You
download it, make a profile, spend a few
weeks meeting people, find someone you
genuinely like, and then delete the app
forever. From your perspective, it's
perfect. The product has done exactly
what you hired it to do. From the
company's perspective, however, it has
just lost an active user, possibly one
who might otherwise have bought a
subscription, paid to see who liked
them, or purchased a temporary boost.
Most businesses do not have this
problem. A coffee shop wants you to
enjoy its coffee so much that you come
back every morning. Netflix wants you to
finish one series and immediately begin
another. Even a gym, despite what
January attendance figures might
suggest, would prefer that you keep
renewing your membership. Dating up to
selling access to a service that
successful customers eventually stop
needing. Their ideal testimonial is
essentially, "This works so well, I will
never have to use it again." That
tension is real, but it doesn't
automatically mean that apps need every
individual user to remain single. A
dating platform still needs success
stories. If nobody ever met anyone, the
app would develop a terrible reputation.
People would stop recommending it and
new users would eventually stop
arriving. Dating apps, therefore, need
to produce enough genuine relationships
to demonstrate that the marketplace
works while also maintaining a large
active population of people still
searching. The business is not built
around permanently retaining every user.
It is built around keeping the overall
marketplace alive as individual users
enter, match, leave, break up, return,
or get replaced by new generations of
singles. And despite the cynicism around
them, dating apps clearly do create
relationships. In a 2022 survey, one in
10 partnered American adults said they
met their current partner through a
dating app. Among partnered adults under
30, the figure was 1 in five. So, the
apps are not simply elaborate slot
machines in which nobody wins. People do
meet, date, and form relationships. The
economic question is not whether the
apps ever work. It is what kind of
system emerges when a company must help
you some users leave while earning money
from the process of everyone else
continuing to search. To understand that
system, it helps to stop thinking of a
dating app as a piece of software and
start thinking of it as a marketplace.
The app itself does not create the thing
people are looking for. It creates a
place in which people can find one
another. In that sense, it is closer to
eBay, Uber or Airbnb than it is to an
ordinary subscription service. The
product is the network of the other
users. This creates what economists call
a network effect. The more relevant
people who join the platform, the more
useful it becomes to everyone already on
there. A dating app containing 20 people
is not particularly useful, even if it
has beautiful animations and a perfect
logo. An app with thousands of active
users nearby, in the right age range,
and interested in the same kind of
relationships is far more valuable. The
relevancy is important here. A den
company might advertise it has millions
of users worldwide, but someone living
in Sydney does not gain much benefit
from the presence of an extra million
users in Brazil. The useful network is
local, filtered, and fragmented. It
depends on distance, age, sexuality,
relationship intentions, activity
levels, and the preference of the people
involved. A platform can be enormous in
total and still feel strangely empty for
a particular user. This is why dating
apps face a difficult version of the
classic marketplace cold start problem.
People will not join an app with no one
to meet, but there will be no one to
meet until people join. A new food
delivery app can sometimes solve this by
paying restaurants and drivers to sign
up. Dating apps cannot simply order
50,000 compatible single people from a
supplier. They need to attract them,
keep them active, and ensure that the
marketplace does not become so
imbalanced that one group receives no
attention while another becomes
overwhelmed. Giving the app away for
free is a major part of solving that
problem. Free users are not merely
customers who have not paid yet. They
are part of the product that makes the
platform valuable to everyone else.
Every active profile adds potential
liquidity to the market. That that is
the possibility that someone opens the
app and finds a person they might
genuinely want to meet. Charging
everyone before they could even browse
would shrink the network, which would
make the service less valuable, which
would make even fewer people willing to
pay. And that is the logic behind the
premium model. The basic marketplace
remains accessible for free, while a
smaller group pays for additional
control, information, or visibility. You
might pay to see people who have already
liked you, send more likes, undo a
decision, apply additional filters,
appear more prominently, or signal
unusually strong interest. The free
product allows you to participate in the
market. The paid product promises to
make the participation less slow, less
uncertain, or less competitive. This is
a subtle but important distinction.
Dating apps cannot directly sell you a
relationship because another person
still has to choose you. What they can
sell is a better position from which to
search. This can still generate a huge
amount of money even when only the
minority pays. 35% of Americans who had
ever used online dating had paid for a
platform or additional feature at some
point. In 2025, MatchGroup reported
approximately $3.49 billion in total
revenue with Tinder alone generating
$1.86 billion in direct revenue. Bumble
reported roughly $966 million in total
revenue for the same year. These
companies closely track metrics such as
paying users and revenue per payer
because the model does not require every
user to subscribe. It requires a large
free network and a smaller number of
people willing to spend within it.
Advertising exists on some of these
platforms, but direct payments are much
more central to the economics of the
largest dating businesses. That means
the user is not simply providing
attention to advertisers in the way they
might on a social media platform. Two
people can use the same app in the same
city for the same amount of time and
have completely different experiences.
One opens the app to dozens of likes and
finds the main problem is sorting
through them. Another receives very
little attention and begins wondering if
anyone is being shown their profile at
all. And there's research which captures
this difference. Among people who had
recently used online dating, 64% of men
said they had at least sometimes felt
insecure about receiving too few
messages compared with only 40% of
women. At the same time, 54% of women
said they had sometimes felt overwhelmed
by the number of messages they received
compared with 25% of men. These figures
describe American users across multiple
platforms and should not be treated as
universal rules. Experiences differ
substantially by app, age, sexuality,
location, and what people are looking
for, but they illustrate an important
economic feature of dating markets.
Excess demand and excess supply can
exist at the same time depending on
where a user sits within the
marketplace. This is not quite the same
as selling an ordinary product. If a
thousand people want the same concert
ticket, the venue can raise its price or
add another show. Attention in dating
cannot be produced so easily. A popular
profile has a limited amount of time and
interest to distribute regardless of how
many people swipe right. Sending that
person 500 additional likes does not
create 500 additional conversations. It
often creates a larger queue from which
they become more selective. Online
dating is therefore a matching market,
not simply a market in which more demand
is always better. A very successful
transaction requires mutual selection.
You might be interested in someone, but
your demand only matters if they are
interested in you. This makes attention
more valuable than the raw numbers of
profiles. 10,000 people who you do not
want or who do not want you are
economically less useful than a singular
match. Research using data from a large
online dating service found a pronounced
hierarchy of desiraability within
heterosexual dating markets. Both men
and women tended to contact people who
were, by the researchers measure, around
25% more desirable than themselves, and
the probability of receiving a response
declined as the gap between the sender
and recipient grew. The researchers were
not claiming that every person has an
objective romantic score. Their measure
was based on patterns of attention
within the platform. The point is that
attention was structured and unequal
rather than randomly distributed. Once
attention becomes unequal, users
behavior can amplify the imbalance.
Someone receiving very few matches may
respond by liking more profiles because
each individual swipe has a low expected
return. But when large numbers of users
adopt that strategy, the people
receiving those likes are flooded with
weak signals. They cannot tell whether
someone is genuinely interested or
simply approving almost everyone. They
respond by becoming more selective,
which lowers the first group success
rate even further and gives them even
more reason to cast a wider net. It
almost resembles a crowded job market,
which applicants begin submitting
hundreds of nearly identical
applications. Employees receive so many
that they spend less time on each one
and introduce harsher filters.
Applicants then receive fewer responses,
so they submit even more applications.
What is rational for each individual can
make the overall marketplace worse. This
helps explain why dating apps place
limits on free behavior and sell
stronger signals. A limited number of
likes forces users to spend attention
more carefully. A super like rose or
similar feature creates a cost signal
because you cannot send an unlimited
number without paying. The recipient can
reasonably infer that it means more than
an ordinary swipe. Whether people enjoy
receiving those signals is another
question, but the economic logic is
clear. When ordinary attention becomes
abundant, platforms can create premium
versions of attention by making it
scarce. The algorithm sits in the middle
of all this. The details of modern
recommendation systems are proprietary,
so strong claims about exactly how an
individual app ranks people should be
treated cautiously. A major 2012 review
of online dating concluded that the
evidence available at the time did not
support the grand claims some companies
made about their ability to predict
long-term romantic compatibility from
profile information. Algorithms have
obviously developed since then, and apps
now have much more behavioral data. But
independent evidence validating specific
commercial matching systems remains
limited because researchers generally
cannot inspect the full models or test
them against long-term relationship
outcomes. They can see which profiles
you pause on, whom you like, who likes
you, whether matches become
conversations, and whether you return
tomorrow. That is useful for predicting
future app behavior. It is much harder
to predict whether two people will enjoy
sitting across from one another for
three hours, resolve disagreements well,
or still be compatible 5 years later.
The app has abundant data about choosing
profiles and relatively little data
about what happens after people leave
it. This matters because the swipe is
not merely a convenient way to display
profiles. It changes the kind of
decision users are making. Selecting a
potential partner used to involve a
messy combination of conversation,
context, mutual friends, repeated
exposure, and social consequences.
Swiping compresses that into a fast,
private, and reversible judgment. There
is always another profile immediately
behind the current one. So, the cost of
projection feels close to zero. That low
cost makes the app easy to use, which is
good for growth. It also creates a high
volume decision environment in which
users can move from considering people
to processing profiles. The difference
is subtle. At the beginning of a
session, you might be asking, "Could I
enjoy meeting this person?" After enough
swipes, the question can quietly become,
"Is this profile good enough to stop me
from seeing the next one?" A series of
three studies published in 2020 found
evidence of what the researchers call a
rejection mindset. As participants
viewed more dating profiles, they became
increasingly likely to reject later
options. Across the studies, the average
chance of accepting a profile fell by
about 27% from the beginning to the end
of the task. The researchers connected
this to declining satisfaction with the
available pictures and pessimism about
dating success. The experiments had
limitations. They focused on
heterosexual participants aged 18 to 30
and cannot perfectly reproduce every
real app. But they suggest that
abundance can change the standard by
which each new person is judged. Earlier
experimental work produced a related
finding. Giving online datas more
potential partners encourage more
searching but could reduce the quality
of the final choice. More options
theoretically increase the chance that a
highly suitable person exists somewhere
in the pool. They also increase the
amount of comparison required and make
it easier to imagine what an even better
person is who's just one swipe away.
This is where the psychology of swiping
meets the economics of the platform. An
effectively endless feed creates option
value. Choosing to focus on one person
means giving up the possibility of
discovering someone apparently better.
In economic terms, the perceived
opportunity cost of commitment rises
when alternatives remain constantly
visible. Even after a good match, the
marketplace does not disappear. It
remains on your phone, fully stocked and
requiring almost no effort to reopen.
The certain timing of matches also
encourages repeated checking. Most
swipes produce nothing immediately.
Occasionally, one becomes a match, a
message, or a date. Comparisons with
gambling and variable rewards are
common, including from people involved
in designing swipe based interfaces.
However, it is worth being precise. That
does not prove every dating app is
clinically addictive or that companies
can directly control romantic rewards in
the way a casino controls the machine.
Matches depend on other human beings.
What the platform does control is the
rapid sequence of decisions,
notifications, and uncertain feedback
surrounding them. The app does not
necessarily need to hide good matches
from you to keep you engaged. The
structure of the market can do much of
that work naturally. Mutual attraction
is rare. Timing is inconsistent. Users
have different intentions and
conversations frequently go nowhere. Add
a large menu of alternatives and people
may become selective, less responsive,
and less willing to invest in a single
interaction. A platform can generate a
great deal of activity without
manufacturing failure because human
dating already contains enough
uncertainty. So, do dating apps want you
to fail? At the individual level, not
necessarily. A user who finds a
relationship is evidence that the app
works. They may recommend it to friends,
return after a future breakup, or
strengthen the company's brand by simply
saying, "We met on Hinge or Tinder or
Bumble." Successful couples are valuable
marketing because they answer the
biggest concern any new user has,
whether spending time on the app could
lead to anything real. At the level of
the business model, however, the
platform is not financially rewarded for
finding every user the best possible
partner as quickly as possible. Public
dating companies report metrics such as
active users, paying users, engagement,
revenue per payer, and subscriber
conversion. They do not primarily report
the average number of days required to
find a satisfyingly long relationship.
That does not establish malicious
intent. Companies report what they can
reliably measure and monetize, but it
reveals the basic incentive structure. A
dating app needs the experience to be
successful enough where people continue
believing in the marketplace, but not
necessarily efficient in the way an
individual user would define efficiency.
The user wants the smallest number of
swipes required to meet the right
person. The platform benefits when the
search lasts long enough to create
engagement and for some users a reason
to pay. Those goals overlap because a
completely useless app will lose its
audience. But they do not overlap
perfectly. Even the phrase designed to
be deleted fits within this model. An
app can genuinely focus on relationship
seeking while still operating a
profitable subscription business. It
does not require one person to remain
subscribed forever. It needs a
continuing flow of new users and enough
time between signup and deletion to
generate revenue from some of them. In
2025, Match Group reported strong growth
at Hinge. Even though Hing's entire
brand is built around eventually leaving
it, the product can celebrate exits
because the business operates across
millions of overlapping searches rather
than relying on one permanent customer.
The more realistic criticism is
therefore not that dating companies
secretly prevent all successful
relationships. It is that the measurable
goals of the platforms can favor
activity over resolution. More swipes
are easier to measure than better dates.
More matches are easier to measure than
an emotionally healthy relationship. A
subscription purchase is visible
immediately. Couple becoming genuinely
compatible over several years is not.
The gap can shape design without
requiring a room full of executives
asking how to keep people lonely. A
product team trying to increase daily
activity might want to add more
notifications. A revenue team might want
to test a payw wall. A recommendation
team might optimize for the probability
that someone swipes, matches, or sends a
message. Each decision can be reasonable
in isolation. Together, they can produce
an experience that is extremely good at
sustaining search and less clearly
optimized for helping people stop
searching. Dating apps have still solved
a genuine economic problem. They
dramatically reduce the cost of
delivering people outside of your
existing social circle. They are
particularly valuable for people whose
dating pool is small, geographically
dispersed, or difficult to identify
online. They allow users to signal
availability and basic preferences
before anyone has to approach a stranger
at a bar and discover several awkward
minutes later that the stranger is
married. But reducing the cost of
searching has not eliminated the
difficulty of choosing. In some ways,
it's just transferred the problem. The
old limitation was access. You could
only meet the people who happen to cross
your path. The new limitation is
attention. You can access far more
people than you could ever seriously
evaluate. And that right there is the
central paradox of the dating economy.
The platforms become valuable by
offering abundance, but abundance can
make users less satisfied with
individual options. They need large
networks, but large networks create
congestion and unequal competition. They
need free access to build the
marketplace, but then have to convert
some of the inconvenience within that
platform into premium features. They
need users to form relationships, but
their revenue is generated while those
users are still searching. They need you
to feel that success remains possible,
that the next session might be more
productive than the last, and that
paying could improve your position.
Sometimes that process ends in exactly
what you wanted. Sometimes it ends with
you deleting the app for a much less
romantic reason. The most accurate way
to understand them is not as evil
machines designed to keep everyone
single or as neutral match makers
concerned with only finding love.
They're marketplaces attempting to
balance user success, network growth,
and monetization at the same time. And
the uncomfortable part is that the
balance which works best for the
marketplace may not always be the
balance which works best for the person
using it.
