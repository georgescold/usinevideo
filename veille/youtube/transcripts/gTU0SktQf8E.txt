Some of the most frequent questions that I 
get asked about on this channel have to do
with personality tests. Things like, "How 
are they different? What are they actually
measuring? And which one is the most reliable and 
accurate?" And since trait theory is one of my
absolute favorite topics in all of psychology, 
today I want to answer those questions using
two of the most well-known frameworks, which 
are the five factor model of personality or
big five and the MyersBriggs type indicator or 
MBTI. Now these two approaches definitely come
from very different psychological traditions but 
interestingly some of the personality tendencies
that they measure actually do overlap. So let's 
go ahead and dive into them both and then I'll
explain how they relate to one another 
and get to all of the other questions.
So first we have the five factor model which is 
one of the most widely accepted frameworks in
modern personality psychology altogether. 
It describes personality using five broad
trait dimensions that reflect stable patterns 
in thinking, feeling and behaving which you
can remember through the acronym ocean. Openness 
to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion,
agreeableness and neuroticism. In this framework, 
each of the traits represents a spectrum.
So people can score higher or lower along each 
dimension rather than needing to fit into a single
category. But what are these categories actually 
assessing? And how are they measured? Well, the
scores in the traits are most commonly determined 
using tools like the Neo Personality Inventory,
which breaks each major trait into six smaller 
components known as facets. But let's look at
the broader dimensions. The trait openness to 
experience is measuring curiosity, imagination,
and receptiveness to new ideas and perspectives. 
It reflects a person's interest in intellectual
exploration, creativity, aesthetic appreciation, 
and willingness to engage with unfamiliar
concepts or experiences. Conscientiousness is 
measuring things like organization, reliability,
and goal-directed behavior. It reflects how 
individuals regulate themselves in order to plan,
follow through on responsibilities, and pursue 
long-term objectives in a structured and
disciplined way. Now, extroversion is measuring 
social energy and engagement with the external
world. It captures the degree to which a person 
is oriented towards social interaction, activity,
stimulation, and the expression of positive 
emotion in interpersonal settings. Agreeableness
is measuring interpersonal orientation, including 
tendencies related to cooperation, empathy,
trust, and concern for others. It reflects how 
individuals approach relationships, particularly
in terms of compassion, social harmony, and 
responsiveness to the needs of other people. And
neuroticism is measuring emotional sensitivity and 
the tendency to experience psychological distress.
It reflects the stability and reactivity of a 
person's emotional system, including how strongly
and frequently individuals experience emotions 
related to stress, worry, and mood fluctuation.
And because the Big Five measures traits along 
continuous scales, it allows us to describe
personality with a lot of nuance. But let's go 
ahead and move on to the MBTI, which takes a
very different approach. I think something that a 
lot of people don't know is that it was actually
inspired by ideas from the Swiss psychiatrist 
Carl Jung who believed that people tend to favor
different ways of perceiving information and 
making decisions. The test was of course later
developed by Meyers and Briggs but that is where 
the original idea came from. Now, in this model,
instead of measuring traits along a spectrum, 
it sorts people into personality types based
on four psychological dimensions that include two 
possible preferences each. When these preferences
are combined, they produce 16 possible personality 
types represented by letter combinations such as
INTJ, ENFP, or ISTP. So, let's look at all four 
of these dimensions and talk about what they mean.
So first we have introversion versus extroversion. 
This dimension measures where people primarily
direct their energy. Introverts tend to focus 
inward drawing energy from reflection and internal
thoughts. Whereas extroverts focus outward gaining 
energy from interacting with people and engaging
with their environment. Next we have sensing 
versus intuition. This measures how people take
in information. Those with a sensing preference 
tend to focus on concrete factual details
and practical realities. Whereas those with an 
intuition preference focus much more on patterns,
possibilities, and abstract connections. Now, the 
third dimension is thinking versus feeling. This
measures how people make decisions. Thinking 
types prioritize objective analysis, logic,
and consistency, whereas feeling types 
prioritize personal values, harmony,
and the impact of their decisions on other people. 
And lastly, we have judging versus perceiving,
which measures how people interact with 
the outer world and structure their lives.
Judging types prefer organization, planning, and 
closure, seeking to have things settled. Whereas
perceiving types prefer flexibility, spontaneity, 
and keeping options open. But again, it's the
combination of preferences from all of these 
dimensions that end up giving you your four-letter
MBTI personality type. So, these two tests really 
are pretty distinct. But like I said earlier,
even though they come from very different 
theoretical backgrounds, several of their
dimensions do overlap. For example, both models 
directly measure extraversion versus introversion,
which is the most obvious. But another 
overlap appears between the big five trait
openness to experience and the MBTI dimension 
of intuition versus sensing. Now, these aren't
identical traits, but people who score high 
in openness tend to enjoy things like ideas,
imagination, and novelty, which does resemble 
the intuitive style that's described in the MBTI.
There's also some weaker relationships between 
agreeableness and the MBTI feeling preference,
as well as between conscientiousness 
and the judging dimension of the MBTI,
which also relates to organization and structure. 
However, there is one very important personality
dimension that the MBTI does not measure very 
directly at all, which is neuroticism. And this
means that two people could have the exact same 
MBTI personality type, but still differ widely in
how anxious, moody, or resilient they are. So 
in other words, while the MBTI can accurately
tell us about our preferences, it can't tell 
us much about how we handle emotions or stress,
which definitely does have an influence on how we 
think and behave. So because of these differences,
psychologists usually view the big five as the 
more scientifically robust model of personality,
which is why we use it so often in peer-reviewed 
research. I think one of the biggest issues with
the MBTI is that it forces people into categories. 
Even though in real life, personality trades
tend to exist along continuous spectrums. For 
example, many people fall right near the middle
of introversion and extroversion instead of being 
one clear type. But the test doesn't allow you
to reflect that. You have to pick either or. The 
test has also faced a lot of criticism regarding
reliability because sometimes people will take 
the test multiple times but then are assigned
different personality types every single time they 
take it. Still, I wouldn't say that the MBTI is
entirely pseudocience. I do think it has value. I 
think it can help us with self-reflection and help
us to understand our preferences. And I also think 
it's a great tool for helping us understand other
people. I love when people tell me they're MBTI 
type because it automatically helps me know where
they're coming from. And I have always personally 
found the test to be very accurate in my own case.
My MBTI type has never changed the dozens of times 
I've taken the test over the years. I am an ENFP.
I have been an ENFP every single time I've taken 
the test. And I fit that description 100%. But
even still, I wholeheartedly agree that the MBTI 
lacks the reliability, validity, and predictive
power of traitbased models, which is why for true 
psychological research, the big five really is
the gold standard. So, I truly hope that this
