# Science of Attraction, Compatibility & Romance | Dr. Paul Eastwick | Huberman Lab
# Podcast Summaries · 147 vues · 00:12:53
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ieEncdmdTJ8&t=302s

In this podcast episode, Dr. Andrew
Huberman hosts Dr. Paul Eastwick, a
psychology professor at the University
of California, Davis, to explore the
science behind attraction, mate
selection, relationship formation, and
compatibility. Their conversation
challenges many common beliefs about
dating and relationships, particularly
those rooted in evolutionary psychology
and market-based perspectives.
They discuss the dynamics of initial
attraction, the influence of dating
apps, the complexities of long-term
relationships, and what truly sustains
romantic bonds over time. The dialogue
also highlights how modern social
behaviors and cultural shifts impact
romantic experiences. Dr. Eastwick
critiques simplified evolutionary and
market-based theories of dating, which
often equate partner selection to a
competitive marketplace based on
perceived mate value, such as physical
attractiveness or financial status.
He explains that while these theories
may apply to initial attraction
scenarios, like meeting for the first
time, they fail to account for how
attraction and preferences evolve when
people spend time together.
Unlike the rigid numerical rankings used
in experiments, such as assigning
numbers on foreheads to simulate
desirability, real-life attraction
includes variability and personal
idiosyncrasies, allowing people to
perceive unique qualities in others that
consensus rankings miss.
The forebrain's capacity for complex
thought, kindness, and strategic
benevolence contradicts the harsher,
survivalist conditions often implied by
evolutionary narratives.
The podcast dives into how people
internalize perceptions of their
desirability and how that influences
interactions, especially in adolescence
and early adulthood.
The mate value concept is experienced
palpably in settings like junior high
dances or social events, where peer
social hierarchies impact who interacts
with whom.
However, adults differ in their
responses to these social cues, with
more mature individuals prioritizing
personal preferences over social
consensus. Eastwick discusses phenomena
like mate choice copying, where people
base some of their attraction on who
else is attracted to someone, but argues
that meaningful relationships often
develop from unique shared experiences
and conversations, rather than external
validation.
The process of slowly accumulating
moments that reveal personality traits
and build trust is crucial in forming
attraction beyond initial appearances.
The conversation touches on the
disproportionate attention that certain
individuals receive on dating apps,
creating an unequal and highly skewed
marketplace dominated by a small set of
popular users.
However, Eastwick emphasizes that
offline social environments are far less
extreme in this regard.
On average, people only agree about
attractiveness about two-thirds of the
time, meaning there is ample room for
variability in partner preferences.
The apps also favor communicative, witty
individuals, especially in texting,
which can give hyperverbal people an
advantage early in interactions,
potentially skewing partner selection
processes compared to face-to-face
dynamics focused on listening and
nonverbal cues.
The apps operate to maximize engagement,
often by attracting more women to
balance the larger male user base,
creating gender-differentiated swiping
behaviors aligned with traditional
evolutionary predictions. Dr. Eastwick
and Dr. Huberman discuss how men and
women differ in how they derive social
and emotional support, with women
typically having broader social networks
and men relying more heavily on their
romantic partners for intimacy and
connection.
This difference explains why men often
appear more eager for relationship
commitment and why women initiate
breakups more frequently.
Men's friendships tend to be more
activity-based and less emotionally
intimate, highlighting challenges men
face in cultivating diverse support
networks.
Eastwick stresses the importance of men
building social bank accounts of
support, even if it is primarily through
shared activities, to improve well-being
and relationship satisfaction.
The podcast explores the power of
storytelling and personal narratives in
forming attraction and intimacy.
Partners often bond over unique shared
stories, inside jokes, and small moments
that reveal personal values and
vulnerabilities.
This depth of connection goes beyond
surface traits like attractiveness or
status, and involves reciprocal
self-disclosure.
Eastwick highlights the value of deeper
questions and conversations, such as the
36 questions approach, to facilitate
getting beyond small talk and fostering
emotional closeness.
While many dating scenarios focus on
impressing others with traits, true
connection arises from genuine curiosity
and shared experiences. Eastwick
discusses how perceived similarity
between partners matters more than
actual similarity in traits or values.
People tend to find commonalities with
those they like, motivated by a desire
to sustain the relationship.
Even when couples differ in politics or
preferences, they often downplay
mismatches if those differences do not
threaten the core bond.
Actual measured similarity predicts
relationship outcomes only weakly,
suggesting that motivated perceptions
and selective attention play a larger
role in relationship satisfaction.
This explains why matching purely on
similar attitudes or demographics, as
some dating algorithms attempt, often
fails to predict relationship success.
Contrary to traditional stereotypes that
prefer younger women and women prefer
older men, Eastwick's research finds
that when given choices, both men and
women show a somewhat equal preference
for younger partners.
The typical age gap seen in couples
arises more from who enters the dating
pool and social conventions than from
fundamental attraction patterns at the
initial dating stage. Later choices,
such as settling down, may reflect
factors like intentions to have children
rather than purely age-based
preferences. The conversation critiques
the notion of love at first sight or
instant connection, asserting that most
relationships form through a slow
accumulation of positive impressions
over weeks or months.
Early impressions tend to be moderate
rather than intense, with desire and
attraction growing as people gather more
information about each other's
personalities, values, and behaviors.
This gradual process helps reduce
uncertainty and build accurate knowledge
of a potential partner.
Text messaging favors quick wit and
verbal fluency, which may disadvantage
those who communicate better
face-to-face or through attentive
listening.
While texting can alleviate social
anxiety by allowing individuals to craft
responses, it also biases partner
selection toward hyperverbal
individuals.
Eastwick cites research showing that
couples' texting styles tend to converge
over time, suggesting mutual adaptation.
However, texting alone is insufficient
to recreate the nuances of in-person
interactions crucial for long-term
compatibility. Participating in social
groups and shared activities, whether
sports, improv classes, hiking clubs, or
community gatherings, provides important
opportunities for developing authentic
connection outside of superficial
initial encounters.
These group contexts allow people to
experience each other's values,
kindness, and behaviors in naturalistic
settings, fostering attraction and
compatibility.
Non-work and non-dating specific
activities offer low-pressure
environments to meet diverse people and
build relationships gradually. Contrary
to common belief, Eastwick emphasizes
that financial status is similarly
valued by men and women when seeking
partners.
Men care about women's financial
prospects as much as women care about
men's.
However, more important than income is
whether partners share a sense of
purpose and manage their time to nurture
the relationship. The negative
psychological impact of unemployment,
particularly for men, may stem more from
identity loss than from financial
concerns alone. Overall, objective
socioeconomic indicators have only
modest effects on relationship
satisfaction. Physical and sexual
intimacy emerge as critical components
for relationship satisfaction and
stability.
Feeling that one's partner is a good
lover correlates strongly with positive
perceptions of the relationship.
Though sexual desire fluctuates due to
life circumstances, it can often be
rekindled through focused effort and
quality time.
The presence of physical intimacy fuels
self-esteem and strengthens emotional
bonds. Absence or severe decline in
intimacy often cascades into broader
relationship challenges. Being in a
committed relationship typically
triggers a cognitive bias called
derogation of alternatives, where
potential alternative partners are
perceived as less desirable, reinforcing
bond stability.
While attraction to others outside the
relationship is natural and not
inherently problematic, acting on those
attractions or secretive communications
can threaten the relationship.
Interestingly, some research shows that
fantasizing about others can increase
desire for one's own partner,
illustrating complex dynamics underlying
fidelity and attraction. Social media
platforms such as Instagram increasingly
serve as massive cloaked dating
environments where direct messaging
drives much user engagement.
This increased connectivity blurs
boundaries and can increase
opportunities for alternative
attraction, thereby challenging
monogamous stability.
The capacity for following up with
strangers online potentiates risks not
present in the past, making social media
potential attack vector for committed
relationships. Awareness and management
of social media use are essential for
relationship health. Breakups often
invoke feelings of failure due to
cultural narratives expecting till death
do us part commitments.
Yet, Eastwick encourages reframing past
relationships as valuable chapters that
contributed to personal growth and
identity.
Maintaining positive regard for
ex-partners, avoiding negative gossip,
and carrying forward lessons supports
emotional health. Relationships that
start early and allow partners to grow
through key developmental milestones
together often build strong shared
narratives, which become sources of
resilience.
Despite pervasive stereotypes, research
shows men and women generally want
similar qualities in partners, including
kindness, support, shared values, and
emotional connection.
Differences in stated preferences, such
as men emphasizing attraction more and
women emphasizing earning potential
more, are less pronounced or absent when
observing real-world partner selection
or speed dating outcomes. Amplified
gender divisions in dating often stem
from cultural narratives and online
interactions rather than actual mate
preferences.
The loss of traditional social
structures and increased reliance on
technology have altered how young people
meet and form relationships.
Eastwick advocates for increasing
in-person interaction through organized
social activities that encourage
vulnerability, shared experiences, and
repeated exposure to potential partners.
These environments resemble the small
group settings of ancestral social life,
supporting the gradual building of trust
and attraction.
Organizing or participating actively in
community events can facilitate
connection and counteract social
isolation. The episode highlights how
texting favors certain communication
styles, often privileging people who are
witty and quick with words, particularly
men, which may disadvantage those who
excel at face-to-face listening and
emotional attunement.
This skew can affect who progresses in
early stage dating, sometimes creating
mismatches between texting success and
long-term compatibility potentials
discovered in person.
Eastwick shares that many new generation
students express frustration with modern
dating, feeling overwhelmed by
technology, reduced socialization, and
skepticism about traditional
relationship narratives.
Despite these challenges, activities
that cultivate in-person human
connection remain critical.
He stresses the importance of patience,
vulnerability, and engaging with others
authentically, even amid a landscape
shaped by technology and shifting social
norms.
