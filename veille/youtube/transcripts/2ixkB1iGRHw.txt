# Why don't dating apps work better? | Joanna Montgomery | TEDxSt Albans

For as long as humans have existed,
we've tried to hack love.
The ancient Greeks brewed love potions
from wine, herbs, sometimes even animal
organs,
hoping that one potent sip would
convince their unwitting suitor to fall
for them.
Medieval matchmakers looked to the
stars, trusting astrology to predict
their perfect match.
And in Victorian England, they turned to
scent, with women dabbing on pheromone
perfumes designed to turn a man's head
and tilt the odds of romance in their
favor.
Today, of course, we have dating apps,
compatibility quizzes based on our
personality archetypes.
We have AI relationship coaches to help
us navigate conflict, and if we can stay
in love long enough to procreate, we
even have robots to rock our babies to
sleep so that we don't have to.
Our methods have changed over time, but
the urge to somehow shortcut loving
another person remains.
But it never quite works, does it?
Bad dates still happen,
breakups still hurt,
grief still wrecks us.
And yet we persist on this mission, on
this quest as old as time, and
underpinning all of our efforts is this
one relentless belief
that connection can somehow be easier.
That if we just find the right tool,
trick, or timing, perhaps it won't all
be as hard.
I know this tension well because I spent
most of my adult life designing tools to
try and make love less difficult.
When I was 21, I built something in my
bedroom that sends the sound and feeling
of your heartbeat to someone you love
when you can't be with them, wherever
they are in the world.
Using it feels a bit like putting your
head on someone's chest.
It was only ever meant to be my
university project, but it went viral on
the internet, and today, 15 years later,
it's still used in over 80 countries
around the world, including in
children's hospitals and in palliative
care.
And the thing that continues to amaze me
is the world has changed so much in that
time.
We've had the rise of smartphones,
wearables, virtual reality, so many
innovative advances, but that piece of
technology, with its one simple feature,
hasn't changed.
And I've come to realize it hasn't
changed because it didn't need to.
The world around us continues to evolve,
but the same cannot be said for our
emotional needs.
So why do we keep trying to hack love?
Why the relentless pull to orchestrate
something so fundamentally human, this
primal urge we're all born with?
Well,
it's because uncertainty makes us deeply
uncomfortable.
Psychologists call it uncertainty
reduction theory. When we care about
something, especially something
unpredictable, we try to control it.
We want to predict it, to mitigate the
chances of damaging it or losing it.
Love, well,
love is pure uncertainty.
It's emotional.
There are no guarantees.
And so we try to protect ourselves. We
want the intimacy, but without the risk.
And who can blame us because the risk is
high.
MRI scans have shown that the brain
processes grief the same way it
processes physical pain.
Whether you lose a child or your husband
of 20 years cheats on you, that pain is
not happening in your imagination. It
happens in your body.
Our connection with the people we love
is the most meaningful, but also the
most terrifying thing in our lives.
So it's only natural that we would do
anything within our power to try and
make that experience more predictable.
The exponential rise of technology has
in many ways exacerbated this desire.
We live in a world now obsessed with
optimization.
We track our sleep, our steps, our
stress. Entire industries have been
built around optimizing how we live,
work, and connect with each other.
So it's no surprise that this has
extended to love, too.
But yet again,
it's not working.
We have more dating options than ever,
yet people are lonelier than ever
before.
We can communicate in an instant, yet so
many of us feel constantly
misunderstood.
We share our lives more publicly than
ever, and yet struggle to form genuine
connection and find happiness in
private.
And with this abundance of tools and
options comes something else. It's
called decision fatigue.
The more choices we have, with so many
ways to connect at our fingertips, the
harder it is to commit and the less
satisfied we feel with whatever we
choose.
In trying to make love easier,
we've managed to make it worse for
ourselves.
So why does love resist optimization?
Why does hacking it never work?
It's actually incredibly simple.
Love isn't logical.
It has no predefined patterns, no known
constants.
Love needs three things: time,
repetition, and attention.
And these are precisely the things that
optimization tries to strip away.
Add to this that we live in a modern
world that has convinced us that if
something is hard or inconvenient, then
it's broken, or at least ripe for
fixing.
So when love doesn't feel easy or
effortless, we treat it like a glitch in
the system.
But the truth is,
meaningful connection requires effort.
That's what gives it weight.
Real connection is risky,
sometimes messy,
sometimes even boring.
It's someone sitting with you when
there's nothing left to say,
reaching out to that friend even though
it feels a bit awkward.
It's deciding to try again for the kids.
It's making the packed lunch day after
day.
Love resists our attempts to control it
because it's not a process.
It's not even a feeling.
It is a practice.
It is repetition.
It is compromise,
and we cannot shortcut it.
There is another reason love resists
optimization,
and this one's biological.
Because despite our desire to eliminate
it, we need the uncertainty.
There's a concept in neuroscience called
reward prediction error.
Our brain doesn't release dopamine just
because something feels good.
It releases it when something feels
better than expected.
That's why the early stages of love
feels electric,
because it's unpredictable, unscripted,
full of those tiny surprises.
The text message, the hand reaching for
yours, a glance,
your baby's first smile.
We feel the rush precisely because we
didn't see it coming.
So when we try to optimize love to make
it more efficient and predictable and
convenient, we strip away the very thing
that made it feel exciting in the first
place.
We flatten the highs in pursuit of
control,
and in doing so, we dull the reward.
The result of this, of all our attempts
to streamline the process, is an
experience that feels perhaps safer,
but ultimately emptier.
I've had the privilege of working at
some of the most vulnerable ages of the
human experience.
The premature babies that might not make
it,
the tiny bodies of toddlers recovering
from heart surgery.
I've watched sobbing adults tenderly
clutching the crepe paper hand of a
dying parent, and I've seen, time and
time again,
what people reach for when things are
fragile.
And in those moments, the moments that
strip life down to its very core,
people don't want better features or
gadgets.
They don't want the shortcut.
That thing I made 15 years ago hasn't
changed because what people want hasn't,
either.
We all want the same thing today as we
wanted hundreds of years ago, to feel
close to someone who matters.
Because at our core, we're not built for
efficiency.
We're built for connection.
We are reaching, sensing, feeling
creatures wired to attune to one
another.
It's why we cry at films.
Why we wince when someone else gets
hurt.
We don't and we cannot measure these
moments in data.
We just know when they matter.
I'm often asked if I think technology
will change how we love one another.
The biggest threat to human connection
is not technology.
It's our persistent belief that it
should be easy.
It's thinking we can walk away from an
argument and swipe right on someone new.
That algorithms and automation will do
the difficult bits of parenting for us.
That someday, eventually, something will
come along that irons out some of this
messiness of being human.
So, what do we do with that?
The question becomes not whether love
should be easy, but what we do with its
difficulty.
We stop asking,
"How can I make this easier?"
and asking,
"What makes this matter?"
Real connection isn't messaging faster
or letting software make our next
decision. It's rarely even a binary yes
or no.
It's what's left when we sit in the bits
that we'd rather skip.
The future we're building
is not about moving faster, it's about
being slower and more intentional with
our efforts.
Change is inevitable,
but meaning lies in what endures.
Because when the moment comes,
and it always comes,
we don't reach for a shortcut. We reach
for each other.
Thank you.
