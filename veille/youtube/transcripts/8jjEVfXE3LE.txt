# This Is Why Your Relationships Don’t Work | Alain De Botton

Most of us, when a relationship ends
badly, arrive at a relatively similar
conclusion about what went wrong. The
other person was not right for us. They
were emotionally unavailable, or too
demanding, or dishonest, or simply not
who we thought they were. The
relationship failed because of what they
were, their particular flaws, their
specific limitations, the gap between
who they presented themselves to be and
who they turned out to be. And this
conclusion is not always wrong. There
are genuinely difficult people in the
world. There are people who are, for
various reasons rooted in their own
history, unable to sustain the kind of
closeness and honesty that love
requires. There are bad matches and bad
timing and bad luck. But I want to sit
with a different possibility, a more
uncomfortable one, and one that I think
contains considerably more explanatory
power for those of us who have found
ourselves in more than one relationship
that followed a recognizable arc, that
started with real feeling and gradually,
almost inexplicably, fell apart. The
possibility is this, that the problem is
not only, or even primarily, in the
people we have chosen. That there is a
pattern operating beneath the level of
conscious awareness, a pattern in what
we are drawn to, in what we find
compelling, in what we interpret as
love, that is quietly, reliably
generating the same outcomes, regardless
of who the other person happens to be.
And that until we understand this it
into the light where it can be examined
honestly, no amount of choosing more
carefully, or trying harder, or hoping
for better luck, will fundamentally
change what keeps happening. There is an
experience I suspect will be familiar to
a great many people. You meet someone
and there is a feeling immediate,
strong, hard to account for by anything
as simple as their appearance or their
obvious qualities. Something about them
grips you. The conversation has an
electricity to it. You find yourself
thinking about them afterwards in a way
that feels involuntary, as if the
thinking is being done to you rather
than by you. There is a sense of
recognition, as if you have in some
obscure way already met this person, as
if they fit somewhere they have no
reason yet to fit. Things begin well.
There is warmth and excitement and the
particular pleasure of early intimacy,
of another person turning towards you
with interest and attention, of being
seen or at least of believing you are
seen. And then slowly things begin to
change.
Not dramatically, not at first, just a
gradual shift in atmosphere, small
disappointments accumulating, a distance
opening that neither of you quite knows
how to close. Old anxieties resurface.
Familiar arguments begin to repeat. The
person who felt at the beginning like an
answer starts to feel by degrees like a
version of the problem and eventually,
with grief and confusion, it ends.
And then some time later you meet
someone else. They are different, of
course, different in appearance,
different in personality, different in
all the ways that seem on the surface to
matter. You are careful this time. You
have learned from the last experience.
You are paying attention and yet a year
or two in something begins to feel
recognizable. The same anxiety in a
slightly different register. The same
distance opening in a slightly different
way. The same argument with different
words. And you begin to wonder with the
particular disquiet that repetition
produces whether the common denominator
in these relationships might in some
uncomfortable sense be you. The most
common response to relationship failure
is to focus on selection. We chose the
wrong person. The solution therefore is
to choose better. To be more careful,
more rigorous, more clear-eyed about red
flags and compatibility and what we
actually want. And there is some wisdom
in this.
Self-knowledge about one's needs and
preferences is genuinely useful. The
capacity to notice warning signs early
is not nothing. But this approach has a
a significant limitation which is that
it treats our choices as if they were
fully conscious and fully rational. As
if the process of being drawn to someone
was something like a decision
subject to improvement through better
information and clearer criteria. And
the experience of most people does not
really support this picture. We do not
choose partners the way we choose
appliances by listing requirements and
systematically evaluating options
against them. We fall. We are drawn. We
find ourselves wanting people in ways
that precede and often override our
rational preferences. The heart, as has
been observed many times and in many
languages, does not take instruction
very easily. What this means is that the
question of why our relationships do not
work cannot be answered purely at the
level of conscious choice.
It has to be answered at a deeper level,
at the level of what drives the wanting
itself,
of what shapes our sense of what is
compelling,
of what registers in us as love before
we have had time to think about it
carefully.
And when you examine this level
honestly, what tends to emerge is not a
random scatter of preferences, but a
pattern, a specific traceable pattern
that is not the product of our adult
reasoning, but of our earliest emotional
education. We tend to think of
attraction as something that happens
between two people in the present, a
kind of chemical reaction generated by
the encounter itself, by the particular
combination of this person and this
moment.
And there is something real in this. The
present encounter matters. But what I
want to suggest is that our experience
of attraction is never purely present
tense. It always also contains echoes of
the past.
The people we find compelling, the
people who feel in that almost
inexplicable way right for us tend to
feel right precisely because they
reproduce something familiar, something
that our nervous system has been
calibrated over many years of experience
to recognize as the emotional texture of
love. And here is where it becomes
important to be very clear about what we
mean by familiar. Familiar does not mean
comfortable, familiar does not mean safe
or easy or pleasant, familiar means
known. It means matching a pattern that
was laid down early under conditions
that had very little to do with our
adult preferences and everything to do
with the emotional dynamics of the
environments in which we first learned
what love looked like. Those
environments were, for most of us, our
families of origin. And what we learned
there about what love feels like, about
how it is expressed and withheld, about
what a person must do or be in order to
receive it, was not learned consciously,
not chosen, not arrived at through any
process of deliberate thought. It was
absorbed through thousands of
repetitions over years of total
emotional dependence on the people
around us. We took in a set of lessons
about love that became so deeply
embedded that we no longer experience
them as lessons at all. We experience
them as simply what love is. Let me be
specific, because abstraction can make
this idea seem more theoretical than it
is. Consider someone who grew up with a
parent or both parents who were
emotionally inconsistent.
Sometimes warm, sometimes withdrawn,
sometimes available, sometimes
unreachable. A parent who was loving in
certain moods, but distant or critical
in others, so that the experience of
being with them was always tinged with a
low-grade anxiety and uncertainty about
where things stood, a sense that warmth
might be withdrawn at any moment, a
hyper-vigilance to the other person's
emotional state. This is not an unusual
childhood. In fact, some version of
emotional inconsistency is extremely
common in families. Parents are, after
all, human beings with their own
difficulties and their own histories.
But what a child develops in response to
this kind of environment is a particular
orientation toward love. Love becomes
associated with uncertainty, with
effort, with a need to manage oneself
carefully in order to maintain the
connection with a particular heightened
aliveness that comes from not quite
knowing where you stand. And then this
person grows up and begins to have
romantic relationships.
And what happens not because they have
made a bad choice, not because they are
foolish or self-destructive,
but because their nervous system has
been thoroughly trained is that they
find themselves consistently drawn to
people who reproduce this dynamic.
People who are sometimes warm and
sometimes distant. People whose
emotional availability is intermittent.
People who generate that specific
cocktail of excitement and anxiety that
somewhere below the level of conscious
thought their system has been trained to
interpret as love. Meanwhile, when they
encounter a genuinely available person,
someone who is consistently warm,
consistently present, consistently
honest, the response is often puzzlement
or mild disinterest or a vague sense
that something is missing. There is no
chemistry, they say. There is no spark.
And what they mean, without realizing
it, is that there is no anxiety, no
urgency, no need to manage the situation
carefully, no electric uncertainty about
where things stand. And because the
absence of these feelings does not match
the template of what love is supposed to
feel like, the available person is
disqualified. Not consciously, not
cruelly, not even with much awareness
that a disqualification has occurred,
simply felt to be insufficient. The
concept of chemistry is one that I think
deserves considerably more scrutiny than
it usually receives. We treat it as
self-evidently positive, as the sign
that something real and valuable is
present between two people, as the
indicator that a relationship is worth
pursuing.
And sometimes this intuition is correct.
But I want to suggest that the
experience we describe as chemistry is a
good deal more complicated than the
cultural conversation around it implies.
Strong initial attraction, the kind that
feels immediate and almost overwhelming,
is not always a response to the genuine
qualities of the other person. It is
often a response to familiarity in the
specific sense I have been describing.
A recognition below conscious thought
that this person fits the template, that
they reproduce something emotionally
known.
And if what is emotionally known is a
dynamic that involves anxiety or
inconsistency or the need to earn love,
then what we are calling chemistry may
in fact be the activation of an old
wound. The pull we feel is real,
but what it is pulling us toward is not
necessarily what is good for us.
It may be pulling us toward what is
familiar, which is an entirely different
thing. This is why so many people report
that the relationships that felt most
alive, most intense, most electric were
also the most painful.
The intensity and the pain were not
coincidental. They were generated by the
same source, the reenactment of an old
and unresolved emotional dynamic that
produces excitement precisely because it
is charged with old feeling, old
longing, old need. And this is also why
calm, stable, genuinely available love
so often fails to register as love at
all, at least in the early stages for
people who grew up in environments where
love was unpredictable. The absence of
anxiety reads as absence of feeling. The
consistency, that is, objectively one of
the most valuable qualities a partner
can have, feels subjectively like a kind
of flatness.
The very things that would make a
relationship genuinely healthy fail to
produce the response that the template
requires. There is a concept in
psychoanalytic thought, I am thinking
particularly of the work of Freud, but
also of later thinkers who have
developed these ideas with greater
precision of what is called the
compulsion to repeat. The idea that
human beings have a tendency to recreate
the emotional situations of their early
lives. Not out of masochism, not out of
stupidity, but out of a kind of
unfinished business.
The original situation, the difficult
dynamic with the inconsistent parent,
the experience of conditional love, the
early atmosphere of emotional
unpredictability was never resolved. The
need that was not met was never met, and
something in the psyche continues to
seek resolution, to look for the
opportunity to re-enter the old
situation, and this time somehow get it
right. This is why people who grew up
with emotionally unavailable parents so
often find themselves in relationships
with emotionally unavailable partners,
and then invest enormous energy in
trying to make those partners available.
On the surface, this looks irrational.
Why keep trying to get warmth from
people who do not seem to have it? Why
not simply find someone warmer? But the
logic, when understood from the inside,
is not irrational at all. It is an
attempt at resolution. If I can make
this person who resembles
in their emotional structure the
original unavailable figure finally turn
toward me, finally give me what I need,
finally be available in the way the
original person was not, then the old
wound will be healed. The old need will
finally be met. The tragedy, of course,
is that it does not work this way. The
new person is not the old person. The
resolution of childhood wounds cannot be
achieved by finding their adult
equivalent and trying again. But this
understanding is very difficult to
access from inside the dynamic,
precisely because the dynamic operates
largely outside conscious awareness.
The person does not think, "I am
choosing this difficult partner in order
to resolve my childhood experience of
emotional unavailability." They think,
"I am drawn to this person. I love this
person. I cannot explain it, but it
feels like the most real thing I have
ever felt." The shift I want to propose
is not the one that is most commonly
suggested, which is simply to try harder
to choose better people. That advice, as
I have been arguing, does not address
the level at which the real problem
operates. If the pattern of attraction
is rooted in early emotional experience,
if we are drawn to what is familiar
rather than what is genuinely good for
us, then the exercise of more careful
conscious selection is limited in what
it can achieve. It is working at the
wrong level. What actually needs to
change is not primarily the selection,
but the understanding. Specifically, the
understanding of what drives the
selection, of why certain people feel
compelling, of what the feeling of
chemistry is actually responding to, of
what emotional template is operating
below the surface of one's apparent
preferences. This is the work that is
both more difficult and more genuinely
transformative than any amount of trying
to choose more carefully. It is also
work that requires a particular kind of
honesty that does not come very
naturally to most people, the honesty of
looking at one's own patterns without
immediately exonerating oneself. When a
relationship fails, the instinct is to
focus on what the other person did
wrong. And again, the other person
usually did do things that were wrong.
But the more useful question, the
question that actually has the potential
to change something is, "What drew me to
this person in the first place? What did
I find compelling? And why?
What did this relationship reproduce
that older, earlier experiences had
already established? What did I expect?
And where did that expectation come
from? Awareness in this domain genuinely
changes things. Not instantly and not
completely. The patterns laid down in
childhood are deeply established, and
they do not dissolve in response to a
single insight. But awareness interrupts
the automaticity of the pattern. When
you understand what you are responding
to, when you can recognize in the moment
of attraction that the pull you are
feeling may be toward the familiar
rather than toward the genuinely good
you acquire, a small degree of freedom
that you did not previously have. Not
the freedom to simply override the
feeling, but the freedom to pause, to
question, to bring some conscious
thought to a process that previously ran
entirely on its own. And over time,
something else becomes possible, too. If
the calm, available person has so far
failed to produce the response the
template requires, if consistency and
genuine warmth have felt until now like
the absence of chemistry, it becomes
possible to begin to re-educate that
response. Not by forcing yourself to
feel something you do not feel, which
never works,
but by allowing your experience of what
is possible in love to expand. By
spending enough time in the presence of
something genuinely stable and warm that
the nervous system begins to update its
picture of what love is allowed to feel
like. By discovering, slowly and a
little unexpectedly, that peace is not
the same as boredom, that the absence of
anxiety in a relationship is not a
deficiency, but a gift, and one that
allows other, quieter, more sustaining
forms of pleasure to emerge. We do not
fall in love with the right person. We
fall in love with the familiar person.
With the person who fits the emotional
template that was established before we
had any say in its formation, before we
were old enough to understand what love
was or what we needed from it, or what
it was capable of being at its best. We
fall in love with what we know, with
what our nervous system recognizes, with
the particular emotional texture that
our earliest experiences taught us to
associate with intimacy. This is not a
condemnation. It is a description of a
process that is, in its origins,
entirely understandable. We were
children. We absorbed what was there. We
built our understanding of love from the
materials that were available to us, and
those materials were imperfect because
all human beings are imperfect, and
imperfect people raise children
imperfectly, and the children go on to
love imperfectly in turn. But it is also
not a life sentence. The pattern is not
fixed. The template, though deeply
established, is not immune to revision.
What it requires is the particular kind
of self-knowledge that is difficult to
arrive at alone, the willingness to look
at one's own history honestly, to trace
the connections between what happened
early and what keeps happening now, to
take seriously the possibility that the
problem is not only in the other people
but in the story one has absorbed about
what love is supposed to feel like.
Relationships work in the end not
primarily because two people have found
the perfect match. They work because two
people have done enough of this kind of
honest self-examination to be able to
meet each other with some degree of
genuine clarity to love, not the
projection, not the familiar template,
but the actual person standing in front
of them. That is rare, and it is hard,
and it is always incomplete, but it is
what love at its most serious is
actually asking of us.
