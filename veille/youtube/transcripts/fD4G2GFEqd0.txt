Hi, I’m Michelle Troutman, creator of the My Journey to Love dot com blog, where you don't

When you get onto a dating platform, 
you can feel like you might already
meet a winner. It's a little like 
playing slots. When the signs align,
you get all tingly inside from another 
win -- another chance for love.
I realized this when I wasn't on a dating site -- when I was on a social media platform and a
guy kept "liking" and commenting on my posts. 
That behavior made me like him more. I blame
it on the dopamine high of the "likes" and attention. But when he stopped engaging,
and the feelings faded, I could see more clearly,
which was a real downer when what 
felt like a connection really wasn't.
This is also the way the 
online dating world works.
Dating app creators didn't discover how 
to manipulate your emotions by accident.
They're using clever marketing 
psychology. Like a casino vendor,
they don't really want you to find 
happiness -- they want your money.
The more you pay, the more you 
can play -- you get more access,
like the ability to send endless messages or more effective filtering of mismatches.
I touch upon some of the tactics dating 
apps use to keep you going, like offering up
seemingly endless profiles, in recent videos and an in-depth blog (at My Journey to Love dot com).
Often, like my experience with the "likes" 
on my posts, the unpredictability of what
we'll find (or what awaits us) also triggers a dopamine rush. Our brains are wired to favor
something new over the tried and true. When we're blinded by constant "likes" or apparent interest,
the thrill of the chase leads us 
on. We feed off the validation.
But you're often not really in love -- 
it's more of an infatuation -- lust.
It's really your brain on dopamine. 
If the chase ends abruptly, though,
you might crash and burn. 
The thrill is later gone.
In his book, Influence: The Psychology 
of Persuasion, Dr. Robert Cialdini
focused on six universal behaviors or marketing principles that manipulate us from behind the
scenes. Many of them apply to the psychology behind dating apps. Here's how that looks:
One: RECIPROCITY: Free swipes motivate you to get more through paying for a subscription.
Two: SCARCITY: When you're on a 
free or low-priced plan, often,
your messaging options or access 
to more profiles is limited.
You might fear you're missing out on 
closer matches. So, you pay for more
access. After my free swipes, which didn't bring me closer to my goals, I felt I should upgrade.
Three: SOCIAL PROOF: When dating apps advertise, they often focus on success stories to
prove that could be you, too. For example, "Sarah from New York City got 47 matches!"
Four: LIKING: Lots of "likes" make you 
feel good, but not every one turns into
a conversation. That happened to me when I got one-hundred "likes" and only one date.
Five: AUTHORITY: Dating sites can claim that their methods are backed by science;
eHarmony's in-depth questionnaires are a classic example of this tactic as a way
to weed out people who aren't interested in a long-term commitment like marriage.
Six: COMMITMENT: If you're on a winning streak, dating apps can promote other options to help
you keep the lucky breaks going, like 
higher-tiered offerings that give you
more access. Because when you're already heavily invested, it's harder to get out
So, how do you break the machine?
The 24-Hour Rule: I'm against playing 
games. So, don't use this as an excuse
to play hard to get. When you get a 
match, wait 24 hours to message. This
allows the "lust/dopamine" spike to settle so your "clear-coding" brain can take over.
Intentional Logging: Set a timer 
for 15 minutes. When it dings,
you’re done. Don't let an app run your life.
Truecasting: Stop trying to "win" 
likes. Write a profile that might
shoo away the wrong people and helps you break the dopamine cycle.
I go into this in a recent blog 
I wrote on dating app fatigue.
Generally speaking, online dating 
fosters inauthenticity. Obviously,
we're behind screens. Because of that extra layer,
for better and for worse, online dating 
helps people hide who they really are.
The apps want your money, but you want a partner. 
Don't confuse a chemical high for a soulmate.
Remember my story at the beginning 
regarding a guy who "liked" my posts,
but didn't like me? Even if he didn't really 
like me as a person, the behavioral psychology
behind the "likes" and engagement influenced my reactions. And when I got trapped in the
online dating app game, I felt the same. Just be aware that if you choose to date online,
you risk being played not just by potential partners, but by marketing tactics on these
platforms -- because they're often after 
only a few things -- your loyalty and your wallet.
