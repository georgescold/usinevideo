>> Instead of reflecting a collapse in how
people value relationships,
situationships can be understood as an
adaptation to the conditions of modern
adulthood. The same conditions that make
them attractive are also what make them
emotionally difficult. Research on close
relationships suggests that not knowing
where you stand with someone increases
stress and changes how interactions are
experienced. A big source of this pain
is mismatched expectations. One person
might see it as exploration, while the
other hopes it'll turn into something
committed. Situationships don't hurt
because they're undefined. They hurt
because not knowing where things stand
makes it harder to protect yourself
emotionally. Developmental psychologist
Jeffrey Arnett describes emerging
adulthood as a stage spanning the late
teens through the 20s. It's a period
where people are still figuring out who
they are, dealing with instability and
postponing long-term commitments. Many
of the timelines that once structured
adult life, like career stability,
housing, and marriage, now arrive later
or less predictably for many people.
Data from the US Census Bureau shows
that the median age of first marriage
has risen steadily for decades alongside
longer periods of education and shifting
economic conditions. At the same time,
Western culture places a strong emphasis
on individual autonomy. Intimacy is
still valued, but so is independence
through separate goals, careers,
friendships, and identities. Traditional
relationship models often come with
expectations of merging lives that many
people don't feel ready for or don't
want. So, situationships emerge as a
compromise. They allow emotional
closeness without requiring immediate
life level commitment. If we zoom out,
this begins to look less like individual
failure and more like structural
pressure. Dating apps intensify these
dynamics. In his book, The Paradox of
Choice, psychologist Barry Schwarz draws
on decades of research to argue that
having too many options can increase
anxiety and reduce satisfaction. When
choice feels endless, people become more
concerned with choosing wrong than
choosing well. This helps explain modern
dating rituals like the talking stage
and the soft launch. People are trying
to understand a connection before they
name it because naming it too early can
feel like a risk. It's tempting to
reduce everything to attachment styles,
but that explanation only goes so far.
Attachment theory is often used to
explain situationships by pointing to
avoidant attachment. Early work by John
Bulby and Mary Ainsworth described this
pattern as a tendency to keep emotional
distance and rely less on others for
comfort. In adult dating, this pattern
can show up as a preference for slower
escalation or greater emotional
distance. At the same time, modern
attachment research emphasizes that
attachment patterns are shaped by
context and experience, not fixed
personality traits. Research by
psychologist Samantha Joel and her
colleagues shows that people with
anxious attachment often want closeness
and commitment, but insecurity and fear
of rejection can make it harder to act
on that desire. Situationships often
persist not because one person avoids
intimacy, but because two different
attachment patterns can lock together,
each responding to uncertainty in their
own way. Situationships tend to function
best when uncertainty is temporary and
actively managed. They're more
sustainable when emotional availability
is mutually understood, boundaries are
discussed rather than assumed,
communication is consistent, and changes
in feelings lead to conversation or
resolution. Under these conditions, a
situationship isn't a waiting room for
something real. It is something real,
just not traditional. Problems start
when people avoid making a decision.
When moving forward would mean either
committing or ending things, no one says
what needs to be said. and the situation
drags on. Over time, that uncertainty
can start to benefit one person more
than the other. Public health data shows
growing concern about loneliness and
social isolation. In 2023, the US
Surgeon General formally identified
loneliness as a public health issue,
pointing to weakened community ties and
fewer non-romantic sources of emotional
support. As friendships, neighborhoods,
and extended families play a smaller
role in daily life for many people,
romantic relationships are increasingly
expected to meet a wider range of
emotional needs. When a situationship
doesn't turn into commitment, it can
feel like a personal failure, even when
it reflects broader social conditions.
