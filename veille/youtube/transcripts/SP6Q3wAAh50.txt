All right, so starting off with every new person 
that you meet. We all want the same thing,
right? Everyone you talk to wants to fit in. 
And when you understand that everyone around
you also wants to be liked and be included 
and be understood, you'll start to approach
social situations with way more confidence, 
way more empathy, and way more likability.
However, one of the biggest hurdles that 
get in the way of making friends is we
tend to focus too much on how we are being 
perceived by others. You focus too much on
how you are doing and what they think of you. 
A good sign that you do this is if you have a
really loud inner critic. Like there's a 
voice in your head telling you, “Oh man,
you need to say this,” or, “No, don't say 
that,” or, “Why did you say that? You're
not talking enough. Now you're talking 
too much. They all hate you.” It's awful.
So I want to encourage you to take all of that 
energy that's focused on yourself and criticizing
yourself and fixing yourself, and channel it into 
listening to other people. And when you listen,
I don't want you to just sit there and do nothing. 
There are some practical things that you can do
while you're listening to someone to really show 
them that you're listening. Maintain eye contact,
kind of an obvious one. Nod while they speak. 
Give a little “yeah,” or “wow, really?”
I want you to do it in a way that it's as 
if it was you going through whatever it is
that they were talking about. I want you 
to meet them where they are emotionally.
Now this is a lot easier done when the person 
you're talking to is talking about something that
has a stronger emotion behind it. If they're 
saying something like, “Yeah man, my grandma
died last week.” You wouldn't sit there and be 
like, “Oh, that's too bad.” They’ll continue,
“Yeah man, it really sucks.” You wouldn't 
sit there and go, “Yeah, I bet.” I want to
encourage you to show a little bit more emotion. 
Show some empathy. Try saying it like this:
“Oh my gosh, your grandma died. I'm so sorry. 
What happened?” That will make a huge difference.
The reason this is important is because 
when people feel seen and understood,
and their emotions feel seen and understood, you 
make them feel valuable. You make them feel like
their feelings are important to you. And when 
you have that mutual sense of understanding,
when both of you are on that same level 
emotionally, that's where friendship can
really grow. Friendship grows rapidly out of 
shared experiences. The quicker that you can
identify something that you share, the 
faster the connection will develop.
At work or at school you could ask, “Hey, how 
are you feeling about this class so far?” Maybe
you're in a new environment, “What food do you 
want to try here?” Those are just some questions
you could ask. Based on their responses, 
you meet them where they are emotionally,
and you see if you notice any overlap. Maybe 
you're feeling the same way about the project
or the class. Maybe you were going to order 
the same meal. Maybe you have the same hobbies,
or you know a little something 
about the hobby they mention.
And you never want to lie about these things. 
It's very important to stay true to yourself
and be true to who you are with the things 
that you actually do like and the things that
you actually don't like. You can be confident 
in having a disagreement without being rude.
That's the most important part. You don't have to 
agree. Just focus on the fact that you hear them.
And this can be done in one-on-one situations, 
but also in group settings. Every group sort
of has a social tone: how loudly everyone talks, 
how formally they dress. If the group is relaxed
and casual, wearing sweatpants and sweatshirts, 
very chill, that's a sign that you don't have to
over formalize. If the group is professional 
and really focused on what they're doing,
you may want to avoid being overly silly. Adjust 
your body language to match others. Lean in when
other people are leaning in. When they speak, 
pay attention so you can speak at a similar pace.
I want to make it clear, though, this is not about 
pretending to be someone else. If the group is
wearing sweatpants and sweatshirts, but you want 
to wear a suit, by all means, wear a suit. But
maybe consider wearing one that feels a little 
more casual. It's not about changing who you are,
but just adjusting the volume of who you are 
so it fits the environment that you're in.
Okay, let's move on to part two: starting 
conversations and navigating group
settings. Let's dive in to practical 
methods for starting conversations.
I want to encourage you to use your environment as 
a tool. Most conversations fail because they feel
forced, they feel inauthentic. The easiest 
way to start a conversation naturally is
to comment or ask a question on something 
that you both are currently experiencing.
Some examples: if you are at work or school, “Man, 
that deadline came up quick. How has it been going
for you?” Or if you're at a party or event, 
“Pretty good turnout. How did you get involved
with the group here?” Or maybe you're in a public 
setting, “That's a cool jacket. Where'd you get
it?” These work because you're asking the person 
about something relevant to their present moment.
Another important point is open-ended questions. 
Open-ended questions can’t be answered with a
simple yes or no. Instead of asking, “Did you 
like the movie?” ask, “What did you think about
the movie?” That's a much more open question that 
encourages them to dive deeper into their opinion.
Once you're getting a handle on starting 
conversations and your small talk is starting
to feel better, that might be the time to 
start sharing some small vulnerabilities.
Revealing a little about yourself will encourage 
them to reveal something about themselves too.
For example: “I always get turned around in 
new places. Have you figured out this campus
yet?” Or, “I'm terrible at remembering names. 
Can you remind me of yours?” Or something more
positive, almost like a guilty pleasure: “I 
actually love romantic comedies. Do you have
a favorite one?” People bond over honesty 
and vulnerability way more than perfection.
Being confident in those insecurities is how 
people see your true confidence, your personality,
and ultimately how they trust you—because 
you're trusting them with those insecurities.
Once you've exchanged some of those 
pleasantries, shown a little vulnerability,
and you're starting to gain trust, 
it's probably safe to move past small
talk and go into deeper territory. For 
example, if they said they like hiking,
you could ask, “What's your favorite trail around 
here?” Notice themes in what they talk about. If
they keep talking about their family, work, or 
hobbies, it clearly means they like talking about
it. Focus there. Offer a short story if you have 
one that relates, as long as it doesn’t dominate.
Now let's transition into group dynamics 
and fitting into existing circles as a new
person. When you're joining a group that 
already knows each other, don't rush to
insert yourself. Take a minute to observe the 
group. Figure out who leads conversations,
who sets the humor, who tends to listen 
more than speak. These dynamics matter,
and understanding them prevents you from 
unintentionally clashing with the group's rhythm.
It's totally okay to just observe and 
listen at first. You don't need to come
in full force. Contribute in a way 
that supports the group rather than
competing with it. Speak up as much 
as you feel called to. You don't have
to worry about saying the perfect 
thing—if they're the right people,
it won’t matter. You can offer encouragement 
or agreement to show that you relate.
When you start talking in the group, 
balance attention between individuals
throughout the group. It's easy to focus 
on one person, but spreading attention
helps everyone feel more included. Make eye 
contact with different members as you talk.
If you're talking to everyone at once, 
try to include as many people as you can.
I also want you to use subtle mirroring when 
you're in a group. If the conversation is
high energy and people are laughing and 
joking, keep what you say light, quick,
and fun. If it's serious, stay focused and 
thoughtful. Groups tend to protect their
identity. Matching their energy makes them view 
you as one of them instead of the outsider.
Once you feel good about the group dynamics, you 
can start focusing on strengthening one-on-one
connections within the group. Find chances to 
connect with members you get along with more
than others. In one-on-one settings outside 
of the group, grab lunch or send follow-up
messages. As you develop those personal bonds, 
the group as a whole will feel more welcoming.
All right, now it's time for part three: 
deepening friendships. Now it's time to
take those one-on-one connections and 
turn them into long-term friendships.
Consistency is what builds depth in 
a relationship. Friendships deepen
when you're in contact a lot and you're a 
dependable person. Sporadic or one-sided
efforts rarely sustain long term. Send 
a short check-in message every so often
related to something they mentioned before: “Hey, 
how'd your exam go?” Make sure the relationship
is established enough before doing this—you 
don’t want to come off too strong too soon.
Don't be afraid to send the first message to 
figure out if they're interested in texting
you one-on-one. If they’re not, don’t take it 
personally. You still have value. If you have
a fairly established relationship, you may get to 
the point where you maintain a rhythm: a weekly or
monthly call, a regular group activity. Try to 
remember small details about conversations and
follow up on them later. This shows reliability 
and that you care, which confirms trust.
At this point, talk to them about values, goals, 
and life outside of the shared environment. Invite
them into a new context—introduce them to 
other friends, or bring them into other
hobbies. This is the time the friendship 
shifts from situational to personal.
Start with light vulnerabilities at 
first—embarrassing stories, small failures. Over
time, move into more meaningful struggles, hopes, 
and insecurities. Always match the level they’re
comfortable with. Don’t overshare and put pressure 
on them to match your level of disclosure.
As friendships deepen, handling conflict with 
care is important. Even strong friendships
will have misunderstandings. 
Avoiding conflict weakens trust,
because not talking about it is another form of 
lying. Address issues directly but respectfully.
Say “I felt left out when you did 
this” instead of “You always do this.”
Also be willing to apologize without 
excuses. Forgive small mistakes they’ve made,
and expect the same in return. Friendships are a
two-way street. If someone forgives you 
but won’t forgive back, that’s unhealthy.
Healthy conflict resolution strengthens 
friendships. When you encounter conflict,
think of it as an opportunity to make the 
friendship stronger than years of time could.
There have been times in my own life where a 
friendship went sideways, but by choosing to
fix it and fight for the friendship, it became 
even greater than before. Of course there are
exceptions, but in most cases, I encourage 
people to resolve issues the best they can.
My hope is that throughout this process, you’ll 
find relationships and friendships in your life
that extend past time and circumstances to 
the point that they feel like brotherhood
or sisterhood. The kind where you can 
reconnect after years or miles apart,
and it’s as if no time has passed at all. 
It took me a while to establish those kinds
of relationships, but I promise they’re 
out there for all of us—including you.
