so long as he brought better cattle
across the line next time.
Two years passed the way years do on a
working ranch, full of ordinary weather
and ordinary labor and the particular
quiet satisfaction of a household
finally settled into itself, and the
small room off the back of the house,
swept clean for so long and kept for a
purpose Silas had once feared he would
never see fulfilled, finally held a
cradle again, and then, a few years
later, a second one beside it. And Nora
would tell you, if you asked her plainly
on a quiet evening with the children
finally down and the fire banked low,
that she had spent seven years mistaking
the absence of risk for a kind of peace,
when it had really only ever been the
absence of anything worth calling a life
at all. She kept in the bottom of a
cedar chest at the foot of their bed a
single small piece of unfinished
needlework she had begun as a girl of 20
and never once looked at again for seven
long years. Carried across every move
and every quiet decision since as though
the not looking could somehow undo the
wanting that had made her begin it in
the first place. And in the spring after
her wedding she finally took it out and
finished it properly. Not to replace
anything she had lost along the way but
simply to complete on her own terms this
time in her own kitchen beside a man who
had never once asked her to explain more
of the story than she was ready to give.
Something she had once been too
frightened to see through to the end.
Some people learn early not to want
things. Not because they are incapable
of wanting but because wanting once cost
them more than they ever fully recovered
from and it seemed safer afterwards
simply to stop asking anything of the
world at all. Nora Whitfield spent seven
years mistaking that particular safety
for peace right up until the night she
found herself quietly packing a bag and
half writing a letter home. Prepared to
repeat the very same leaving that had
never once actually protected her from
anything. Only delayed for a good long
while the ordinary and difficult and
worthwhile business of living. She did
not send that letter. That is really the
whole of it when you set it plainly
beside everything else. Not a grand
declaration, not a rescue from anywhere.
Just a sealed envelope left unsent on a
small table and a woman who finally
finished a piece of needlework she had
once been too afraid to complete and a
man on a porch step who was patient
enough to wait nine ordinary months for
her to remember how to want something
largely again.
Thank you for spending this evening with
Nora and Silas. If their story kept you
company tonight I would be glad to have
you back for the next one. Until then
take care of yourselves and keep
believing even when it takes a good
while to remember how.
