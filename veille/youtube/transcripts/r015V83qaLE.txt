Let's start with the Gottman Institute's
groundbreaking work. Doctors John and
Julie Gottman have studied more than
3,000 couples over four decades, and
their findings fundamentally changed how
we understand relationships.
They discovered something remarkable.
They could predict divorce with 94%
accuracy by observing couples discuss a
conflict for just 15 minutes. But they
weren't looking at what couples fought
about. They were looking at how they
fought.
They identified four communication
patterns they called the four horsemen.
Criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and
stonewalling. When these patterns show
up consistently in a relationship, the
research shows that marriages are in
serious trouble. Not surprisingly,
contempt specifically was the single
strongest predictor of divorce.
But even more important is what they
discovered about successful marriages.
In stable, happy couples. The ratio of
positive to negative interactions was
5:1.
Five positive moments for every negative
one during everyday interactions.
When successful couples fight, this
number goes up to 20 positive moments
for each negative one. What this tells
us is that the goal isn't avoiding
conflict. It's about how couples
navigate it together.
Recent longitudinal research from 2024
adds another layer. A 10-year study
following couples found that
relationship satisfaction doesn't
decline uniformly for everyone.
Twothirds of couples who remained
married maintained high satisfaction
levels throughout while only onethird
experienced consistent decline. This
challenges the old assumption that all
marriages naturally lose their spark
over time.
In essence, marital satisfaction is less
about the passage of time and more about
the patterns couples establish and the
effort they make to maintain the
relationship.

So, what does the research, both
established and emerging, tell us
actually works? First, the Gottman
research on bids for connection is
crucial. When your partner makes a small
bid for attention, look at this. How was

three options. Turn toward, turn away,
or turn against. Couples who stayed
together responded positively to these

divorced only 33% of the time. It's
these consistent small moments of
connection that make all the difference.

partnership.

Not couples who accidentally fall into
