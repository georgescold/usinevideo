# The Paradox Of Choice Is Making Online Dating Harder
# Silent Chemistry · 11 vues · 00:06:11
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y9jdFc2PYmw

You open the app. You start swiping. 50
faces in 5 minutes, 100 in 10. Your
thumb moves faster than your brain can
actually process any of them. And
somewhere around match number 30,
something strange happens. You stop
feeling excited. You start feeling
tired. If you have ever wondered why
dating feels harder now than it did for
your parents, even though you
technically have access to more
potential partners than any generation
in human history,
this video has the answer. It is not a
personality flaw. It is not bad luck. It
is a documented psychological mechanism,
and by the end of this video, you will
know exactly what it is called and what
to do about it. In the year 2000, two
psychologists ran an experiment in a
grocery store. They set up a tasting
booth for gourmet jam. One day, they
displayed 24 flavors. Another day, only
six. With 24 flavors, more people
stopped to look, but only 3% of them
actually bought a jar. With six flavors,
fewer people stopped, but 30% of them
bought one. People were 10 times more
likely to commit when they had fewer
options. Psychologists call this choice
overload. Your brain does not experience
more options as freedom. It experiences
them as work, and when the work gets too
heavy, it just walks away.
Now replace jam with people. A 2017
study found that the more options daters
believed they had, the less satisfied
they were, the more uncertain they felt,
and the more they compared.
This is not a coincidence. It is the
exact same mechanism, just running on
human beings instead of jam.
But choice overload is only the first
layer. Underneath it is something
sharper, something researchers call
counterfactual thinking. Here is what
that means.
Imagine you are on a date with someone
named Alex. Alex is kind, funny, and
easy to talk to.
20 years ago, if you met Alex through a
friend, you would probably feel lucky.
But tonight, your phone is sitting on
the table, and you know there are 30
other matches waiting behind the screen.
A study on this exact behavior found
that people who believe they have many
potential partners available start
imagining those alternative partners
more often. They regret their choices
more. They feel less committed, not
because Alex did anything wrong, but
because their brain keeps quietly
comparing Alex to people who do not even
exist yet.
That is counterfactual thinking. You are
not being cheated on. You are being
outcompeted by your own imagination. And
here is the part almost nobody talks
about. This does not require you to
actually act on those other options.
Researchers studying couples found that
people who kept dating apps installed
while already in a relationship reported
lower satisfaction and weaker commitment
than people who deleted them. Not
because they were talking to anyone
else, just because the option was still
visible. That is disposability. When
alternatives are always one tap away,
the person in front of you starts to
feel replaceable, even when you do not
mean for them to. So, you would think
with all this comparison, people would
eventually get pickier and end up
happier.
The opposite happens. Psychologist Barry
Schwartz split decision makers into two
types: maximizers, who need to know they
picked the absolute best option, and
satisficers, who set clear standards and
stop searching once those standards are
met.
Maximizers often end up with objectively
better outcomes, but research
consistently shows they are less
satisfied, more anxious, and more prone
to regret than satisficers. Because when
your options are infinite, there is
always a theoretically better choice you
have not found yet. Perfection becomes
impossible to rule out, so it becomes
impossible to feel settled. And here is
the part that stings the most. When you
only had one option and it disappointed
you, you could blame the world. When you
have 100 options and you are still
disappointed, you quietly start blaming
yourself. You must have picked wrong.
You must be bad at this. Except you are
not bad at this. You are just running a
normal brain through an environment it
was never built for. So, what actually
works? Not deleting every app forever.
Just narrowing what you are optimizing
for. Take 5 minutes and write down three
qualities that are genuinely
non-negotiable for you. Not surface
traits like height or a job title, but
things like how someone treats people
when they are frustrated, or whether
they follow through on what they say.
When someone meets those three, stop
auditioning them against people who only
exist in your notifications. To recap,
choice overload makes you freeze.
Counterfactual thinking makes you
compare. Disposability makes real people
feel replaceable. And chasing the
perfect option instead of the right one
guarantees you will never feel finished
searching. You are not broken. You were
just choosing inside a system built to
keep you swiping, not settling. Next
time, we will look at something smaller,
but just as important. What actually
makes a first message easy to answer and
why most people get it wrong in the
first 3 seconds.
