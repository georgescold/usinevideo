You texted them. They saw it, and then
nothing. No reply. No explanation, just
silence. If you've ever been ghosted,
you know that specific kind of
confusion. That loop in your head
asking, "Did I do something wrong? Are
they okay? Do they just not care?" But
here's what most people never talk
about, the psychology of the person
doing the ghosting. Because ghosting
isn't always cruelty. Sometimes it's
actually something much deeper. Stay
with me. Ghosting is when someone
suddenly cuts off all communication. No
calls, no texts, no explanation, and
completely disappears from your life. It
can happen with a romantic partner, a
friend, a colleague, even family. And
what makes it so psychologically painful
is the ambiguity. When someone breaks up
with you, it hurts, but at least you
know. When someone ghosts you, your
brain keeps searching for answers that
never come. Psychologists call this an
open loop. Your mind keeps replaying the
situation, trying to find closure that
doesn't exist. But what's happening
inside the mind of the person who
ghosts? That's where it gets really
interesting. This is the most common
type. They don't ghost because they
don't care. They ghost because they care
too much about avoiding discomfort. The
idea of saying, "I'm not interested," or
"I need space," feels overwhelming to
them. So, instead of having a hard
conversation, they disappear. Deep down,
they often feel guilty, but the anxiety
of confrontation is stronger than the
guilt. These are people who learned,
usually from childhood, that emotions
are unsafe. When a relationship gets too
intense, too close, too real, they shut
down. Not to hurt you, but because
emotional closeness triggers a deep fear
inside them. Psychologists call this an
avoidant attachment style, and ghosting
is often their unconscious way of
creating distance when they feel
overwhelmed. This person genuinely meant
to reply, but one day became two days,
two became a week, and now it feels too
awkward to respond. They're not
malicious. They're just buried under
anxiety, life stress, or mental health
struggles. And the longer they wait, the
harder it feels to come back. This
person has been hurt before. Maybe they
were lied to, cheated on, or abandoned.
So now, at the first sign of something
feeling wrong, they leave before they
can get hurt again. Ghosting isn't about
you, it's about their wounds. And yes,
some people ghost deliberately. They
lose interest and don't feel the need to
explain themselves. They see
communication as optional. This type is
the least common, but the most painful
to experience. When you get ghosted,
your brain reacts almost the same way it
reacts to physical pain. A 2011 study
from the University of Michigan found
that social rejection activates the same
neural pathways as physical injury.
Getting ghosted literally hurts, not
metaphorically, neurologically. Your
brain also goes into problem-solving
mode. It keeps looking for the missing
piece, an explanation, a reason,
something that makes sense. And when it
can't find one, anxiety fills the gap.
You start questioning yourself, "Was I
too much? Not enough? Did I say
something wrong?" This is called
rumination, and it can spiral into low
self-esteem, trust issues, and even
symptoms of anxiety or depression if
left unchecked. And here's something
nobody tells you, the pain of ghosting
isn't just about the person who left.
It's about the story your brain tells
you about why they left. Social media
and dating apps have changed the rules.
When you have 50 conversations open at
once, people stop feeling like people.
They become options. And options are
easier to drop without explanation.
Psychologists call this the paradox of
choice. The more options we have, the
less we value each one. There's also the
screen effect. Sending a difficult
message in person feels very different
from just not replying to a
notification. Technology has made it
physically effortless to disappear. And
when something is effortless, more
people do it. First, and this is
important, resist the urge to keep
checking their profile. Every time you
do, you reopen the wound. Second, close
the loop yourself. Your brain wants
resolution, so give it one. Write a
message you'll never send, Journal about
it. Talk to a friend. Create closure
internally because it may never come
externally. Third, remember this. How
someone treats you in their worst moment
says nothing about your worth and
everything about their emotional
capacity. You were not ghosted because
you were too much. You were ghosted
because they didn't have the courage to
stay. And that's not your loss.
