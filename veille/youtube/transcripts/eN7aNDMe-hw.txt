# Should Anyone Build a Dating App in 2026?
# ItsMadeByDani · 3 vues · 00:03:08
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eN7aNDMe-hw

Is it worth creating a new dating app?
Not if you can build one. That's easy.
The real question is, would a better
dating app change anything? So, I
decided to analyze dating apps as
products, not experiences, and see where
the system breaks. This isn't a personal
story. This is a product analysis. I
looked at incentives, user behavior,
monetization models, and repeated design
patterns across platforms. And one thing
became clear very quickly. Dating apps
are not failing by accident. Dating apps
are optimized for one thing, to keep
users active. Common mechanisms across
platforms are showing 99 plus likes
without context, notifications that
suggest interest but don't confirm it,
temporary starting boost to spike
visibility, and ambiguous signals like
someone viewed your profile. These
mechanics don't bring clarity and
uncertainty is why people keep coming
back. That's not matchmaking. That's
engagement design. Then comes
monetization. Premium features don't
improve connection. They grant access to
information. But exposure isn't
compatibility. Many users discover that
a large portion of likes are irrelevant,
distance or intent don't match, and that
visibility doesn't lead to
conversations. At that point, you're
already invested, so you stay. Another
pattern shows up repeatedly when
attention is controlled by invisible
algorithms. Silence feels like
rejection. Lack of feedback feels
personal and comparison becomes
constant. Dating apps turn human
interactions into metrics. Any system
that ties self-worth to peak numbers
creates unnecessary psychological
pressure. Not because people are
fragile, but because the system doesn't
provide any explanation. Here's the
fundamental issue. Dating apps succeed
when people stay. Users succeed when
they leave. That's a structural
contradiction. A product optimized for
attention cannot be optimized for
resolution. Even with better matching,
less ghosting, more transparency, and
fewer pay walls. Business model still
rewards time spent, not outcomes. So, I
went and asked the obvious question.
What if we fixed all of it? Imagine no
fake likes, clear intents, anti-
ghosting mechanisms, slower but more
deliberate matching, and minimal
gamification. On paper, it sounds ideal.
In practice, it clashes with how many
people actually use these apps. People
say they want relationship, but often
use these apps for validation,
attention, and optionality. It's not a
flaw in people. It's the reality. So, is
it worth creating a new dating app?
Maybe. But you have to accept one thing.
You're not just designing a software.
You're designing incentives for human
behavior. And unless those incentives
change, the outcome won't. Maybe the
next solution isn't an app at all, but
something outside the engagement
economy. So before building a dating
app, ask, are you solving connection or
monetization friction? And that question
applies to a lot more than dating.
