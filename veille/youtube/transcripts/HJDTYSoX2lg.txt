# The 4 Behaviors That Predict Divorce With 94% Accuracy (Gottman's Four Horsemen)
# Sara Jung Psychology · 887 vues · 00:04:04
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJDTYSoX2lg

A researcher watched couples argue for
15 minutes and predicted whether they'd
divorce with 94% accuracy. He wasn't
looking at what they fought about. He
was looking at how. The Love Lab,
Seattle, 1986. His name is John Gottman,
psychologist, University of Washington.
He built an apartment on campus, wired
it with cameras and heart rate monitors.
Couples came in, lived there, argued
about real things. Gottman watched every
frame. Over four decades, he studied
more than 3,000 couples and he found
four patterns. Four specific behaviors.
When all four show up, the marriage is
almost always over. What you think kills
relationships? Most people think affairs
and marriages or money problems or one
massive screaming match. They don't.
Those are symptoms, not the disease. The
disease is quieter. It lives in your
kitchen on a Tuesday night. It's an eye
roll, a sigh, a sentence that starts
with you always. Gottman called them the
four horsemen of the apocalypse.
Horseman number one, criticism.
Criticism is not a complaint. There's a
difference and it matters. A complaint
targets a specific behavior.
You forgot to take out the trash.
A criticism targets the person. You
never think about anyone but yourself.
Hear the shift? One is about the trash,
the other is about your character.
Criticism uses absolutes. You always.
You never. It turns a problem into a
verdict. The antidote. Gottman calls it
the gentle start-up. Instead of you
never help, try I'm feeling overwhelmed.
I need help tonight. I feel, not you
are. That's the entire difference.
Horseman number two, contempt. This is
the worst one, the single greatest
predictor of divorce. Contempt is
disgust directed at your partner. It
says, I am better than you. It shows up
as sarcasm, mockery, name-calling,
hostile humor. But mostly, it shows up
as the eye roll. That tiny gesture
carries more destructive power than
screaming. Gottman even found contempt
predicts physical illness. Partners who
receive contempt get sick more often.
Their immune systems weaken. Contempt
doesn't just kill marriages, it damages
bodies. The antidote, build a culture of
appreciation deliberately, daily.
Gottman found a magic ratio, five
positive interactions for every one
negative. Five to one. That's what
stable couples maintain. Contempt
thrives below that line. Contempt grows
when you catalog failures. Appreciation
grows when you scan for what they do
right. Horseman number three,
defensiveness. Your partner says, Did
you pick up the kids like you said you
would?
You say, I was slammed at work. You know
my schedule. Why didn't you do it?
That's defensiveness. It sounds like
self-protection. It's actually a
counterattack. Every time you deflect,
your partner hears one thing, your
feelings don't matter to me. The
antidote, accept responsibility, even
just for your small part. You're right,
I forgot. I should have called. I'm
sorry.
You don't take all the blame. You just
stop playing tennis with it. Horseman
number four, stonewalling. This is the
shutdown, the withdrawal, the blank
stare at the wall while your partner
talks. No eye contact, no nodding, no
response.
You've left the conversation without
leaving the room. 85% of stonewallers
are men. Gottman measured it. And it's
not apathy, it's flooding. The heart
rate spikes past 100 beats per minute.
The body goes into fight or flight and
you choose flight while sitting
perfectly still. The antidote, take a
break, but do it the right way. Tell
your partner, I'm overwhelmed. I need 20
minutes. Then I'll come back. Then
actually come back. That's the part most
people skip. The diagnosis. Here's the
uncomfortable part. You recognized
yourself in at least one of those. Maybe
two, maybe all four. That's normal.
Every couple uses these sometimes. The
difference between couples who last and
couples who don't, the ones who last
repair. They catch the eye roll. They
rephrase the criticism. They come back
after walking away. Relationships don't
end because of one big fight. They end
because of four small habits that happen
so often you stop noticing them. Now you
know what they are, which means you can
actually stop. Subscribe for more.
