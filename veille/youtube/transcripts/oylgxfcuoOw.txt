Are you an introvert or an extrovert?
Most people think they know, but most
people are actually wrong.
We use these words to explain why we
feel drained after social situations,
why we prefer silence over stimulation,
or why some people seem to come alive in
groups while others come alive alone.
But somewhere along the way,
introversion and extroversion became
labels instead of explanations. And in
doing so, we started misunderstanding
what personality really is. So today, I
want to clarify something fundamental.
Introversion and extroversion are not
about how social you are. And they are
not personality types in the way we
casually use the word. They are about
how your nervous system relates to
energy. And this matters across
cultures, including in South Asia.
Someone will say, for example, I'm an
introvert because I dislike people or
I'm an extrovert because I get bored
alone. But introversion and extroversion
were never meant to describe how much
you like people. They were never meant
to describe social skill or confidence
or charm. And they definitely were never
meant to explain why someone is too
quiet or too loud. They describe ways in
which human beings manage their energy,
stimulation, and contact with the world.
And the reason this matters so much is
because many people spend their entire
lives trying to correct something in
them that was never broken in the first
place.
Seeing how this is not about
personality, we need to start by looking
at very common misconceptions.
Introverts are often misunderstood as
shy, being distant or withdrawn, whereas
extroverts are often misunderstood as
superficial, loud, or attention-seeking.
But both interpretations miss the point
entirely. Introversion and extroversion
are not about personality style. They're
about where you recharge. So let's
clarify what we actually mean by
personality. When we talk about
personality style or personality type,
we're usually referring to traits and
characteristics such as how expressive
you are as an individual, how cautious
or bold you tend to be, how emotionally
intense, organized, flexible, sensitive
or assertive you are. These are patterns
of behavior and temperament. They
describe how you move through the world.
Whereas extroversion and introversion
don't describe how you behave. They
describe how you recover. They're not
about whether you're warm or reserved,
whether you're confident or hesitant,
articulate or quiet. You can be an
introvert with a very strong presence.
And you can be an extrovert who is very
thoughtful, measured, and emotionally
deep. Personality traits tell us what
you're like, but introversion and
extroversion tell us what restores you.
That distinction matters because someone
can have an outgoing personality style
and still be introverted in terms of
energy. They may speak very easily, lead
meetings, enjoy connection, and then
need long periods of solitude to
recalibrate. And someone can have a
calm, understated personality style and
still be extroverted in terms of energy.
They may not dominate conversations, but
they may feel more alive, focused, and
grounded through interactions with
others. When we confuse personality
traits with energy orientation, people
end up mislabeling themselves and then
building lives that quietly exhaust
them. An introvert doesn't lose energy
because of people. They lose energy
because of too much stimulation. That
stimulation can come from noise, from
constant conversations, from having to
respond, react, engage, perform, or just
stay mentally on. Even positive
stimulation can be exhausting if it's
continuous. Whereas an extrovert doesn't
need people because they are empty
inside or something. They need
interaction because it activates them.
Engagement helps regulate their inner
world. So being in contact helps
organize their thoughts and emotions.
And once you understand that, so many
lifelong confusions begin to make so
much sense, such as why someone can love
people deeply and still need long
periods of solitude. and why someone can
enjoy being alone but start to feel
dull, restless, or invisible without
interaction. From a personality science

portraits, including extroversion and
