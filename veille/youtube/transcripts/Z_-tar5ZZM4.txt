# The Death of Dating Apps | Why Tinder, Bumble & Hinge Are Failing
# Quillette · 49 k vues · 00:06:48
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z_-tar5ZZM4

Picture this. You're looking for love.
So, you opened an app, uploaded your
best photo, maybe the one from that
wedding 3 years ago, wrote something
clever in your bio, and started swiping.
And swiping. And swiping. Welcome to the
most efficient romantic experience ever
designed and possibly the worst one in
human history.
Let's start with the numbers because
they are brutal. Bumble, once valued at
$30 billion, has seen its share price
collapse more than 19% from its 2021
peak. Match Group, which owns both
Tinder and Hinge, has shed billions in
market cap and laid off 13% of its
workforce. In the UK, Tinder lost
600,000 users in a single year.
And a Forbes Health study found that 79%
of Gen Z users reported dating app
fatigue. And a 2025 study found that 77%
of Gen Z adults currently in
relationships met their partner not
through an app, but in person. The
industry's response? Well, Bumble's
founder, Whitney Wolfe Herd, returned to
the company she'd left and admitted
publicly that dating apps are rooted in
rejection and judgment, and that these
are not healthy dynamics. The woman who
built the thing is telling you they're
not working.
So, what went wrong? In 1975, a
biologist named Amotz Zahavi put forward
what he called the handicap principle.
The idea is deceptively simple. For a
signal to be honest, it has to be
costly. A peacock's absurd tail works as
proof of genetic fitness precisely
because it is wasteful and dangerous.
Any bird can grow a few feathers. Only a
genuinely healthy bird can sustain that
kind of spectacular, idiotic display.
The cost is the point. The waste is the
signal.
Now, apply that logic to a dating app. A
man swipes right. What has that cost
him? A fraction of a second, a flick of
a thumb, nothing. Research from Queen
Mary University of London analyzed
nearly half a million Tinder users and
found that men liked a large proportion
of profiles they viewed, but received a
match just 0.6% of the time. Women, far
more selective in their swipes, matched
at a rate of 10%. The conclusion? Well,
when swiping is free, men swipe
everything and filter later, or don't
filter at all. This is not a character
flaw, it's economics. When the cost of
sending a signal is zero, the signal
carries zero information. Your dating
profile is not a real signal of your
fitness, your personality, or your
interest. It is a photograph taken on a
good day, assembled at peak conditions,
and deployed indefinitely. It is, and I
love this phrase, a fossilized signal,
a snapshot of a moment, not a live
performance of who you actually are.
Here's what nobody wants to admit. We
didn't used to need dating apps, not
because people were better at romance,
but because the architecture of everyday
life did the work for us. Sociologist
Ray Oldenburg coined the term third
places, not home, not work, but the
pubs, cafes, bookshops, bowling alleys,
and community halls where people lived
their social lives, and where, as a
byproduct, almost incidentally, people
met each other. The corner bar, the
church social, the workplace canteen.
These were not dating spaces per se,
they were living spaces, and that
distinction mattered enormously. When
you crossed the room to talk to someone
at a pub, risking public rejection,
improvising in real time, reading body
language, you were doing something
costly. You were signaling, you were
saying, "I'm confident enough to try. I
find you interesting enough to risk
embarrassment." A swipe says none of
that. Decades of suburbanization,
car-dependent city planning, and the
commercial death of local gathering
spaces hollowed out these environments.
Dating apps rushed in to fill the gap.
And for a while, it seemed like a
reasonable solution. But it wasn't. It
was a band-aid over a structural wound.
So, what is the industry doing about
this? Well, they're adding AI. Bumble
and Hinge are investing heavily in
AI-driven matching features, smarter
algorithms, better compatibility scores.
The promise of a system so intelligent
it will finally find you the one.
But the problem never was a matching.
The problem was the signaling. Making
the algorithm more efficient does not
fix an information failure. It is, and I
will use this analogy because it is
exactly right, trying to fix inflation
by printing more money. More supply of a
worthless signal is still worthless.
And yet, something strange is happening.
Eventbrite data shows that speed dating
events grew 35% between 2022 and 2023.
In Australia alone, more than 26,000
people attended speed dating events in a
single year. Interest-based gatherings,
book clubs, running groups, climbing
gyms, pottery classes are seeing surges
in participation with people openly
describing them as the places they're
actually meeting people. Why?
Because these formats do something apps
cannot. They introduce trust. When you
buy a ticket to a speed dating event,
choose an outfit, commute to a venue,
and sit across from a stranger and
improvise conversation, you have already
sent a signal before a single word has
been spoken. You showed up and you
risked something.
The discomfort is not a flaw in the
design. It is the design.
None of this means dating apps are
useless. For gay and lesbian communities
in less accepting areas and for people
in rural regions, for those with limited
mobility or social anxiety, they remain
genuinely valuable tools. But as a mass
solution to the problem of human
connection, well, the experiment has
largely failed. Because courtship that
costs nothing communicates nothing. The
signals that actually matter,
vulnerability, effort, the willingness
to be seen and possibly rejected, cannot
be compressed into a JPEG and 150
character bio. Romance has always
depended on a certain productive
inefficiency. On the wasted evening, the
awkward silence, the walk around the
room that might go badly.
That waste, it turns out, was never the
problem.
The waste was the whole point.
