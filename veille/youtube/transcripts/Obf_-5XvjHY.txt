# How Many Personality Traits Actually Exist? (Massive Study)
# Spencer Greenberg · 11 k vues · 00:11:22
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Obf_-5XvjHY&t=116s

A debate has been raging for ages among
personality researchers about a
fundamental question related to human
nature. How many dimensions of
personality are there? In other words,
how many different personality traits do
you need people to be scored on in order
to have an accurate sense of what
they're like? Do you need three numbers
to represent a person's personality? Or
could it be more like four, five, six,
10, or even 100? This might even seem to
you like the sort of question that has
no answer. But surprisingly, it does.
And today we're going to explore what
that answer is. One of the early
attempts to answer this dates all the
way back to 1944, and it relates to what
is today the most famous personality
test, the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator,
also known as MBTI. It scores people on
four traits or spectrums. In particular,
it evaluates where each person falls
between being an introvert and an
extrovert, where they fall between being
a feeler and a thinker, between being
intuitive and a sensor, and between
being a judger and a perceiver. Most
MBTI-style tests then dichotomize the
results. If you're above average on
extraversion, they call you an
extrovert. Otherwise, they call you an
introvert. This dichotomization makes
the test far less accurate because on
each trait, they put you in just one of
two buckets. To give an example, most
people fall near the middle on most of
these traits. For instance, on sensing
versus intuition, if you dichotomize the
data, then you force lots of people who
are right near the boundary to go on one
side or the other, which makes it less
accurate. If we use the same underlying
framework, but we don't dichotomize
people, and instead represent them as
having a score on each of the four
MBTI-style spectrums, we can see these
suggest that there are four important
dimensions of human personality. But is
it really true that personality has four
dimensions? And how would we actually
know? After the MBTI was released, many
other theories of personality were put
forward. Cattell developed a test based
on 16 traits around 1950. Eysenck
developed a test with just two traits
around 1960. If we fast forward a bit, a
major new personality test contender
came on the scene, known as the Big
Five. Whereas MBTI-style tests are based
on the psychological theory about human
nature, the Big Five started with
empirical data. Researchers took all of
the adjectives in the English language
that could be used to describe a person.
They duplicated the list and asked
people, "Which of these adjectives apply
to you?" They noticed the adjectives
tended to group together. If someone
said they were organized, they'd usually
also say they were perfectionistic. If
someone said they were social, they'd
usually also say they were talkative,
and so on. These groupings were then
made more rigorous using a method known
as factor analysis. The idea is that you
run an algorithm that tries to find
hidden dimensions in your data. One of
these dimensions might capture, for
instance, how conscientious you are,
which could include both your level of
organization and perfectionism in one
factor. When they ran these factor
analysis algorithms, they found there to
be five personality dimensions, hence
the name the Big Five. Other researchers
replicated the five dimensions on
different populations using different
personality questions, getting a similar
result. There's some debate over whether
you get these five dimensions in all
cultures. In small-scale societies, for
instance, some people argue you get
fewer factors. But, across most
countries that were studied, these same
five dimensions of personality were
found. So, the Big Five says there are
five primary dimensions of personality.
Whereas MBTI-style tests say there are
four dimensions. How can we tell which
is right? We ran our own study aiming to
answer this question. The idea was to
see whether using five dimensions,
rather than four, enabled more accurate
predictions about people. In the study,
we asked people 37 questions about their
lives, such as how many close friends
they have, how satisfied they are with
their life, and whether they received a
promotion at work. The goal was to
predict the answers to these 37
questions. We also administered each
person a Big Five test and an MBTI-style
test based on the same underlying theory
created by Briggs and Myers. We could
then compare how accurately we could
predict the answers to the 37 questions
using each of the two personality tests.
The results were quite remarkable. The
Big Five test had a prediction accuracy
more than 50% higher on average than the
MBTI style test. But, how do we know
that this is because of having five
factors rather than four? Well, we can
tell because we can see what happens
when we drop from the Big Five one of
its factors. In this case, neuroticism.
Neuroticism is the tendency towards
negative emotionality such as
depression, anxiety, and moodiness. Even
with that factor gone, the Big Five, or
maybe we should call it the Big Four in
this case, still made more accurate
predictions than the MBTI style test by
about 20%. If we add that fifth factor
back to the Big Five, it helps even
more, boosting the accuracy by another
27%. In other words, the five dimensions
of personality win out over the four.
You get a lot more predictive accuracy
using five rather than four factors.
But, is five factors really enough to
capture the major aspects of
personality? In the 2000s, another
contender came on the scene known as
HEXACO. Its creators, Lee and Ashton,
argued based on looking at international
data sets that there was actually a
sixth factor of personality. This sixth
factor is known as H, which stands for
honesty-humility. Low scores in this
dimension tend to be manipulative,
arrogant, greedy, and law-breaking. The
HEXACO model also organizes the other
dimensions of personality a bit
differently than the Big Five, as we can
see in the diagram here. Openness and
conscientiousness stay intact, but some
other components get moved around. We
decided to put the HEXACO framework to
the test by running another study. To do
so, we developed a set of questions that
reflected the scales used in HEXACO. We
then ran factor analysis to confirm that
this set of questions grouped the way
that HEXACO said they should, and indeed
they did. So, HEXACO passed the very
first test. We then had people in the US
take both this test and our 102 question
Big Five test. That way we could
directly pit them against each other.
So, how well did each test fare? Well,
there are two different ways of looking
at this. First is to see how well each
framework did at predicting the answers
to the personality questions themselves.
For example, knowing how conscientious
someone is, you can predict how they
likely answered questions related to
being organized and perfectionistic. We
can capture this idea in terms of the
percent of variation explained by each
personality factor. When we do this, we
get the following result. What we can
see is that across the full set of
personality questions from both tests,
each of the five factors from the Big
Five captures quite a bit of variance in
people's responses. The first factor
captures the most, the second factor
captures the second most, and so on.
But, all five of the first factors
captures quite a lot of variance. But,
after the first five, something weird
happens. We get a substantial drop-off.
The sixth factor, corresponding to H, or
honesty-humility from HEXACO, captures a
lot less variation in responses than
each of the Big Five factors do. To make
matters worse, the seventh factor is
only a little bit worse than the sixth
factor. So, while it seems natural to
stop after five, before the big
drop-off, if you are to go further and
keep six, it seems very hard to explain
why you aren't also keeping seven and
eight and so on. This is not looking
good for HEXACO so far. But, we still
have another important way to look at
this question. Ultimately, to be useful,
personality tests shouldn't just model
how you answered those exact questions
in the personality test. They should be
able to predict other things about your
life. In this case, we collected 66
facts about people's lives, a big
increase from our last study, and we
attempted to use the five scores from
the Big Five, as well as the six scores
from HEXACO, to predict each of these 66
answers. So, how well did each test do?
Remember, when we did a similar test
comparing the MBTI-style test to the Big
Five, we found that that extra fifth
factor added a lot of predictive
accuracy. So, is that what we found in
this case? Actually, no. The two tests
did about the same at predictive
accuracy. HEXACO was only a tiny bit
more accurate. You might think that
using six factors, rather than five
should always improve your prediction
accuracy because you literally know more
about a person. You have six numbers
about them rather than just five. But in
this case, that six number didn't do
much for you. It led to a negligible
increase in prediction accuracy. But
let's look deeper into this. What
specific facts about a person were we
better able to predict using HEXACO than
using the Big Five? Since HEXACO adds
the H factor, honesty-humility, we might
expect that it did especially well at
predicting factors related to morality.
And indeed this was the case. It had an
advantage relative to the Big Five at
predicting whether a person had used
physical force against someone else,
whether they'd been arrested in the last
10 years, and whether they'd cheated on
a romantic partner. Interestingly, as
shown in this diagram, there were
actually more facts where the Big Five
did a better job of predicting them than
HEXACO did than there were the reverse.
The Big Five did a better job of
predicting who had donated to charity in
the past year, who'd been fired from a
job, and who'd received an award. We
believe that our study on HEXACO shows
that for people in the US at least, it
has little to no value above and beyond
the Big Five unless your concern is
specifically on topics related to
honesty or humility, such as moral
behavior. This doesn't rule out the
possibility that HEXACO could be useful
in other countries, but it does cast
doubt on its universality. So, how many
major dimensions of personality are
there truly? Five appears to be the best
answer as science understands it today
in terms of getting the biggest bang for
your buck. Once you know how open,
conscientious, extraverted, agreeable,
and neurotic a person is, you know the
broad strokes of their personality. But
there's a very important caveat here.
This does not mean that there only five
dimensions of personality that exist.
What do I mean by that? Well, suppose
for instance that you're particularly
interested in risk-taking behavior. It's
known that this is linked to the
extraversion dimension of the Big Five.
But if what you really care about is
risk-taking, you can improve your
accuracy at predicting it by adding
another scale to measure risk-taking in
particular. This will do a better job at
predicting risk-taking than if you just
use the broad big five dimensions. You
can actually take this logic even
further. If your goal is simply to make
the most accurate predictions possible,
and you don't care about compressing or
representing that information, then you
don't even need the big five. You can
train a model to predict things using
all the questions in a personality test.
And in fact, it tends to be more
accurate than using the big five. The
big five does not capture everything
there is to know about personality.
There are aspects of personality that
are missing from the big five. And you
can subdivide each of the big five into
narrower facets and then measure those
to get more nuanced results. In fact, we
have a project to try to find every
facet of personality that's missing from
the big five. So, I'll have more to say
about that soon, hopefully. What's so
special then about the big five? It's
that these five factors of personality
capture a ton of information about
people in just five numbers. Those five
scores, in our study for instance,
captured 40% of the variation in
people's responses on individual
questions. So, those first five factors
seem to be special based on how much
predictive juice you get from so little
squeeze. In a sense, there are an
unlimited number of factors of human
personality, but five provides a
surprisingly accurate model that's hard
