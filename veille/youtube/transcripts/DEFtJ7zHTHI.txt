# What Is Stable Matching? (The Algorithm Behind Med School & Dating Apps)
# SysSketch · 17 vues · 00:04:42
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DEFtJ7zHTHI

Every doctor gets a hospital, every
hospital fills its slots, and nobody on
either side wants to swap. Is that even
possible?
Dating apps, job markets, medical
residency, they all share one shape. Two
groups, and every member of each group
ranks every member of the other best to
worst. A matching pairs each person on
one side with exactly one person on the
other. The real question isn't whether
you can pair everyone up, that's
trivial. It's whether the pairing holds
once people can see who else is out
there. Will anyone want to break their
match and pair up with someone else
instead?
That's the problem stable matching
solves, and it shows up in more places
than you'd expect. Hiring pipelines,
school assignment systems, even national
kidney donation registries, all running
some version of the same underlying
idea.
Formally, side A and side B each write
out a full preference list ranking every
member of the other side from favorite
to least favorite. A matching assigns
each person on side A to exactly one
person on side B, using up every slot on
both sides. That's the easy part, any
random pairing satisfies it. The hard
part is what happens after the pairing
is announced. Once everyone can compare
their partner against every other option
they could theoretically have got.
If two people who aren't matched to each
other would both rather switch to each
other than stay put, the whole
arrangement is in trouble, no matter how
neatly it filled every slot on paper.
Here's why this is harder than it looks.
Say Alice is matched with Y and Bob is
matched with X looks fine on paper, but
Alice actually prefers X over Y and X
actually prefers Alice over Bob. Both of
them would rather ditch their current
partner and pair up with each other.
That's called a blocking pair, and it's
the technical definition of failure
here.
A matching is unstable if any such pair
exists anywhere in it. A matching is
stable only when there are zero blocking
pairs, meaning nobody anywhere has both
the desire and the opportunity to
defect.
Randomly pairing people almost always
leaves at least one blocking pair
sitting there, quietly waiting to fall
apart the moment anyone compares notes.
1962, David Gale and Lloyd Shapley
proved a stable matching always exists
no matter what the preferences look like
and gave an algorithm that finds one.
Every unmatched person on side A
proposes to their favorite remaining
option on side B, each B compares the
new offer against whoever it's currently
holding and keeps whichever proposer it
prefers, rejecting the other. A rejected
proposer crosses that name off their
list and proposes to their next choice
instead.
This repeats, propose, compare, reject,
repeat until every single person is
matched.
No coordinator, no central planner, just
one simple local rule applied over and
over until it can't be applied anymore.
Let's trace three rounds with three
people on each side. Round one, Alice
proposes to X, Bob proposes to Y, both
get held. Cara also proposes to X, X
compares Cara against Alice, prefers
Alice and rejects Cara. Round two, Cara
now free proposes to her second choice Z
and Z holds her. Round three, every
single person is matched. Nobody was
rejected this round, so the algorithm
halts right there.
The final result, Alice with X, Bob with
Y, Cara with Z. Three proposals were
rejected along the way and not one
lingering complaint survives anywhere in
the final outcome.
Two things need proving and both fall
out of the same mechanism.
First, termination. Every proposal
permanently crosses one name off
somebody's list and there's only a fixed
number of possible pairs total, so the
algorithm can't loop forever. It runs
out of moves. Second, stability. Suppose
someone ends up wishing they got a
different partner. They must have
proposed to that person earlier and been
rejected, which means that person was
already holding someone they liked more
and from that point on they only ever
trade up.
So that preferred partner never wants
them back either. No blocking pair can
survive the process, which is exactly
why the algorithm always lands on
something stable.
But stable doesn't mean fair to both
sides equally.
Whichever side does the proposing ends
up with the best partner it could
possibly get across every stable
matching that exists for that set of
preferences. That's called being
proposer optimal. The side receiving
proposals gets the opposite deal. The
worst partner it could get across every
stable matching, still stable, just the
least favorable stable outcome available
to it. Flip who proposes and you flip
who benefits, the algorithm itself is
neutral, but the setup you choose isn't.
This is exactly why real-world matching
systems fight hard over who gets to be
the proposing side before the algorithm
ever runs.
This algorithm is already running parts
of your life.
National Resident Matching Program uses
a stable matching process to place
roughly 40,000 doctors in a hospital
residencies across the United States
every single year. New York City and
Boston assign students to public schools
with variants of the same idea. Kidney
exchange programs use related stable
matching logic to chain together
incompatible donor-recipient pairs into
working swaps. Some labor markets and
academic hiring clearinghouses lean on
it, too.
One deceptively simple
propose-and-reject loop published over
60 years ago still quietly clearing real
high-stakes markets today. Thanks for
watching.
