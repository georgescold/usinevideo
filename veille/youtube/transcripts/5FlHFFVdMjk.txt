# Why Do We Attract The Wrong Partners
# TheSpiritualLibrary · 182 vues · 00:03:24
# https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5FlHFFVdMjk

People often repeat the same unhealthy
relationships because the brain is more
attracted to what feels familiar than to
what feels healthy. This isn't weakness.
It's psychology. A study from the
University of Toronto found that people
are two to three times more likely to
choose a familiar negative pattern over
a new healthy one. The brain prefers
what it already knows, even if what it
knows is painful. This is why someone
who grew up around criticism or
emotional distance may feel strangely
comfortable with a partner who ignores
their feelings. It feels normal.
Meanwhile, when someone kind and stable
appears, it can feel confusing or even
too quiet. One client once told her
therapist, "He's so good to me. I feel
uncomfortable." She was uncomfortable
because he was wrong for her. She was
uncomfortable because her body wasn't
used to emotional safety. Yet another
reason people repeat these patterns is
neurochemistry.
Dramatic or unpredictable partners often
create a strong emotional high. A 2019
study found that inconsistent affection
increases dopamine release by up to
400%.
That's why hot and cold behavior can
feel exciting even though it's
unhealthy.
It's the same chemical system involved
in addiction. In contrast, stable
relationships release common chemicals
like serotonin and oxytocin. These
chemicals feel peaceful, not intense. To
someone used to chaos, peace feels like
no chemistry. Attachment research also
explains this cycle. Hazen and Shaver
studies show that people with anxious or
avoidant attachment often choose
partners who match their old emotional
wounds. For example, a person who never
felt enough as a child may keep choosing
partners who make them work for love.
It's not that they enjoy suffering.
Their mind is trying to solve an old
problem with new people. Real life
examples are everywhere. Someone leaves
a controlling relationship, promises
they'll never do again, and then dates
another controlling person with a
different haircut. Another person keeps
falling for emotionally distant
partners, thinking this time I can fix
him. And many people insist, "I always
attract the wrong type, not realizing
they are recognizing a familiar
emotional pattern, not an attractive
one." Breaking this cycle isn't just
about willpower. It requires retraining
the nervous system. Psychology research
on habit forming shows it takes 66 days
to change emotional patterns and about
90 days of consistent healthy
interaction for the brain to become more
interpretable as safe instead of
strange. That means the beginning of a
healthy relationship may feel slow or
unfamiliar, but that discomfort is
actually a sign of growth. Over time,
something shifts. Calm begins to feel
natural. Respect feels normal. Being
valued feels expected instead of
surprising. The same person who once
felt addicted to emotional drama can
learn to enjoy stability.
What once felt boring starts to feel
like peace. In the end, people stop
repeating painful relationships when
they realize one simple truth. The
comfort zone is built from old survival,
not from real love. And when the brain
finally learns that safety can feel
gentle instead of dramatic, the entire
pattern changes. If you enjoy these
